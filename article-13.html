Credit Scores and Mortgage Rates

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

Why your credit score matters for a mortgage

A credit score is a number that summarizes how risky you appear as a borrower, based on your credit reports. Mortgage lenders use it to decide whether to approve you, what loan programs you qualify for, what interest rate to offer and how much mortgage insurance to charge. Because a mortgage lasts decades and the amounts are large, even a small difference in rate adds up to thousands of dollars. Improving your score before you apply is one of the few things that can lower your cost without changing the house you buy.

What goes into a FICO score

FICO is the best-known scoring model, and its scores range from 300 to 850. The company publishes the main factors and their approximate weights.

FactorApproximate weightWhat helps
Payment history35%Pay every bill on time
Amounts owed30%Keep card balances low relative to limits
Length of credit history15%Keep older accounts open and in good standing
New credit10%Avoid unnecessary new accounts and inquiries
Credit mix10%A mix of cards and installment loans can help over time

FICO describes scores of 800 and above as exceptional, 740 to 799 as very good, 670 to 739 as good, 580 to 669 as fair and below 580 as poor. Other scoring models exist, and the labels differ slightly.

Typical minimum scores by loan type

  • Conventional loans: commonly about 620.
  • FHA loans: 580 for the lowest down payment, and lenders may accept 500 to 579 with a larger down payment.
  • VA loans: no official minimum, but lenders set their own, often in the 580 to 620 range.
  • USDA loans: lenders commonly look for around 640.

These are general figures. Each lender can add its own requirements, and rules change, so ask.

How lenders read your scores

Lenders usually pull scores from all three credit bureaus, and for each borrower they typically use the middle score. With two borrowers, many lenders use the lower of the two middle scores. The scores lenders see can differ from the free scores in banking apps, because they may use different scoring versions. The rules on which scoring models lenders may use have been changing, so ask your lender which scores they will use.

Check your reports early

  • Get your free credit reports from AnnualCreditReport.com, the official source. The bureaus currently offer free weekly access.
  • Check every account, balance, address and late payment.
  • Dispute errors with the bureau and the creditor in writing. Bureaus generally have about 30 days to investigate.
  • Do not pay a company to fix real negative items. Legitimate negative information can generally remain for years.

Habits that raise a score

  • Pay on time. Set up autopay for at least the minimum.
  • Lower card balances. Using less of your available limit helps. A common guideline is under 30% utilization, and lower is better.
  • Do not close old cards right before applying. It can shorten your history and raise utilization.
  • Limit new applications. Each hard inquiry can cost a few points temporarily.
  • Resolve collections carefully. Ask how a payment will be reported before paying.

Credit negative items such as late payments and collections generally stay on your report for about seven years, and bankruptcies for seven to ten years, but their impact fades over time.

Shopping for a mortgage without hurting your score

Mortgage inquiries made within a short window are generally treated as a single inquiry by scoring models, so comparing lenders usually does little damage. The window ranges from about 14 to 45 days depending on the model, so try to do all your rate shopping within two weeks. Get Loan Estimates on the same day.

Before you close

Your credit is checked again before closing. Do not open new accounts, take on a car loan, make large purchases on credit or miss payments. Do not move large sums without a record, and avoid changing jobs. Any of these can delay or change your loan.

A timeline for improving your score

  • 6 to 12 months before applying: get your reports, fix errors, set up autopay and pay down card balances.
  • 3 to 6 months before: keep balances low and avoid new credit.
  • 1 to 3 months before: get pre-approved and compare Loan Estimates.
  • Until closing: leave your credit profile untouched.

Questions and answers

  • Does checking my own score hurt it? No. Checking your own reports is a soft inquiry.
  • Can I buy with a low score? Sometimes, using FHA or other programs, but the cost is usually higher.
  • Will paying off a collection raise my score? It depends on the scoring model. Ask before paying.
  • What is rapid rescore? A service lenders can request to update your report after you fix an error or pay down a balance.

Applying with a co-borrower

If two people apply together, the lender reviews both credit histories. Many lenders use the lower of the two middle scores, so a co-borrower with a weaker score can raise the rate even if the other score is strong. Compare quotes with and without the co-borrower if only one income is needed to qualify, and talk through what each person’s debts do to the debt-to-income ratio (see our affordability guide).

Authorized users and thin credit

Some people with little credit history become authorized users on a trusted family member’s well-managed card. Whether this helps depends on the scoring model and the issuer’s reporting. Others build history with a secured card or a small installment loan paid on time. Lenders can also consider alternative data such as rent and utility payments in some cases, so ask if you have a thin file.

If your score is lower than you need

  • Check whether one error or one high balance is the cause, and fix that first.
  • Ask a lender what score and ratios are required for your target loan.
  • Consider waiting a few months to raise your score if the rate difference is large.
  • Ask about FHA or other programs, comparing the full cost including mortgage insurance.
  • Consider a nonprofit housing counselor approved by HUD for free or low-cost guidance.

Common mistakes before applying

  • Opening a new credit card or financing furniture before closing.
  • Missing a single payment in the months before applying.
  • Closing old accounts and raising utilization.
  • Paying a credit repair company that promises results.
  • Not reading the credit report for errors until the lender does.

Final thoughts

A good credit profile is built over months, not days. Check your reports early, fix what you can, keep payments on time and balances low, and leave your credit alone once you apply. Then compare lenders on the same day, and test each offer in the calculator. The effort can save far more than it costs.

How to read a rate quote alongside your score

When a lender gives you a rate, ask which assumptions it uses: the credit score, down payment, loan type, whether points are included and how long the rate is locked. Two quotes are only comparable when these match. If your score is near a tier boundary, such as 620, 680, 700 or 740, ask whether a small improvement could change your price. Sometimes paying down a card or fixing a report error a few weeks before you apply moves you into a better tier, and the saving lasts for the life of the loan. Ask the lender to show the price difference so you can decide whether waiting is worthwhile.

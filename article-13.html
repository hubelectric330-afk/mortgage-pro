Understanding Your Credit Score Before Buying

By A Regular Homebuyer | Updated November 2026 | 12 Min Read

I thought my credit score was fine. I had a credit card I paid off every month, I had never missed a payment, and I had a car loan I had been paying on time for years. When my husband and I started talking about buying a house, I assumed the credit part would be easy. Nobody warned me that "fine" and "great" are very different things when you're applying for a mortgage.

Our loan officer pulled our scores on a Monday morning. I sat across from him with a coffee in my hand, feeling confident. When he told me my middle score was 682, I didn't know what to think. I asked if that was good. He said it was "okay." That was the moment everything shifted. That "okay" score cost us an interest rate that was almost a full point higher than the best available rate. Over the life of our loan, that difference added up to more than $40,000. I was stunned. I thought I was doing everything right. It turned out I had been doing some things wrong without knowing it.

I'm writing this because I don't want anyone else to walk into a lender's office thinking their credit is fine when it could be so much better. The difference between a "fine" score and a great one can be tens of thousands of dollars over the life of your mortgage. Understanding your score before you apply is one of the most valuable things you can do for yourself. This is the story of what I learned and what I wish I had known.

What a Credit Score Actually Is

A credit score is a number, usually between 300 and 850, that lenders use to estimate how likely you are to pay back money you borrow. It's built from information in your credit reports, which are records of how you've handled credit over time. The higher the score, the lower the risk you appear to be, and the better the terms lenders will offer you.

For mortgages, most lenders use what's called a FICO score, and they look at the middle of your three scores from the three major credit bureaus. That middle score is the one that matters. Not the highest, not the lowest, but the middle one. That's why my 682 was the number that stuck.

Here's the part I didn't fully appreciate at the time. The difference between a 682 and a 760 isn't small. It's the difference between a rate that feels like a burden and a rate that feels like an opportunity. Over 30 years, a lower rate can save you more money than a year of your salary. That's not an exaggeration. That's just math.

Your credit score is not just a number. It's a price tag. It decides what your mortgage will cost you every month for the next thirty years. Before you walk into a lender's office, it deserves more attention than you think.

What Actually Goes Into Your Score

When I finally sat down and learned how scores are built, I realized there were things I had been doing wrong for years. Here are the main factors and how much they matter.

1. Payment history (about 35%)

This is the biggest factor, and it's the simplest. Do you pay your bills on time? Every missed or late payment can hurt your score. If you've been paying on time for years, this part is probably fine. But even one late payment can linger on your report for a while. I was proud of my payment history, and this wasn't the problem. But I wasn't getting the full benefit because of other factors.

2. Credit utilization (about 30%)

This is the percentage of your available credit that you're actually using. If you have a credit card with a $10,000 limit and you're carrying a $3,000 balance, your utilization is 30%. This was my biggest problem. I had been using a large portion of my available credit without even realizing it. I thought that as long as I paid my bill every month, I was fine. But the balance I was carrying relative to my limit was hurting my score, even though I wasn't missing payments. The ideal utilization is under 30%, and under 10% is even better.

3. Length of credit history (about 15%)

The longer your accounts have been open, the better. I had closed an old credit card a few years earlier because I wasn't using it, and I had no idea that closing it hurt my score. It shortened my overall credit history and reduced my available credit at the same time. I wish I had known to keep it open.

4. Credit mix (about 10%)

Lenders like to see that you can handle different types of credit, like a mortgage, a car loan, and a credit card. I had a car loan and a couple of credit cards, so this wasn't really a problem for me. But it's worth knowing.

5. New credit inquiries (about 10%)

Every time you apply for credit, a hard inquiry appears on your report, and it can lower your score a little. I had applied for a few store credit cards in the year before we bought the house, and those inquiries had added up. I didn't realize they were affecting my score. The damage from each one is small, but they can add up quickly if you're not careful.

The Numbers That Made Me Take It Seriously

When my loan officer showed me how different scores affected our interest rate, I finally understood why this mattered so much. Here's a rough look at what a score can cost you on a $300,000 loan.

Credit Score Range Approximate Interest Rate Monthly Payment (P&I) Total Interest Over 30 Years
760–850 6.0% ~$1,798 ~$347,000
700–759 6.4% ~$1,877 ~$375,000
680–699 6.8% ~$1,955 ~$404,000
660–679 7.2% ~$2,036 ~$433,000

Look at the difference between the top and bottom rows. Over 30 years, the difference in total interest is more than $85,000. That's not a small amount. That's a college fund. That's a decade of family vacations. That's the difference between a comfortable retirement and a tight one. And the only thing that separated those numbers was a score that most people would call "fine."

Here's the trap: Most people don't know their credit score until they're already applying for a mortgage. By then, it's often too late to fix the problems quickly. The time to check your score is months, not weeks, before you start house hunting. That gives you time to make changes that can actually show up on your report before the lender pulls it.

What I Wish I Had Done Before Applying

Looking back, there are several things I would have done differently. If you're planning to buy a house in the next six to twelve months, here's what I would recommend.

1. Check your credit reports early.

You can get your reports for free from AnnualCreditReport.com. Check all three bureaus, because they don't always have the same information. Look for errors, outdated accounts, or things that don't belong to you. Errors are more common than you'd think. Disputing them can take a few months, so start early.

2. Lower your credit utilization.

If you're carrying balances on your credit cards, try to pay them down before you apply for a mortgage. Even a small reduction in utilization can help your score. Aim to keep your balance under 30% of your limit on each card, and under 10% if you can.

3. Don't close old credit cards.

I made this mistake, and I regret it. Closing old cards shortens your credit history and reduces your available credit, both of which can hurt your score. If you're not using a card, put it in a drawer instead of closing it.

4. Avoid applying for new credit.

In the six months before you apply for a mortgage, avoid opening new credit cards, financing a car, or applying for a store card. Every hard inquiry can ding your score, and the impact is bigger when you're close to a big loan application.

5. Keep paying every bill on time.

This sounds obvious, but it's the single biggest factor in your score. Set up automatic payments if you need to. A single missed payment can undo months of progress.

6. Ask about rapid rescoring.

If you're already applying and your score is borderline, ask your lender about rapid rescoring. It's a process where your lender can quickly update your credit report if you can prove errors or pay down balances. It's not available in every situation, but it's worth asking about.

Checking your credit score before you buy is not about being obsessive. It's about being prepared. A few months of focused effort can save you tens of thousands of dollars over the life of your mortgage. That's one of the highest-return activities available to any homebuyer.

What Happened After We Applied

We ended up buying the house with the rate we were offered. We didn't have time to fix my score before closing, so we accepted a rate that was higher than I wanted. But the story doesn't end there. About two years later, when rates dropped and I had spent time improving my credit, we refinanced. My score had climbed to 752, and we were able to get a much better rate. That refinance saved us thousands of dollars, and it was a direct result of the work I had done after learning what my score actually meant.

The lesson wasn't that I had made a terrible mistake. The lesson was that I had been flying blind. I didn't know what my score was or how it was built, and that lack of knowledge cost me money. Once I understood it, I could actually do something about it. That's the shift I want for you.

What I Tell Friends Who Are Buying Now

When friends tell me they're thinking about buying a house, the first thing I ask is whether they've checked their credit report. Most of them haven't. They tell me their score is fine, and I nod, because I used to say the same thing. Then I tell them what I learned the hard way. That "fine" is not the same as "great." That a few months of focused effort can save them tens of thousands of dollars. That checking your score is not a burden, it's an opportunity.

If you're buying a house, please don't skip this step. Pull your reports, look at your utilization, avoid new credit, and pay every bill on time. If your score is lower than you'd like, give yourself time to improve it before you apply. The house will still be there in six months. The mortgage you sign will be with you for thirty years. The difference between a good score and a great one is worth the wait.

Your credit score is one of the few financial numbers that affects almost every part of your life, from your mortgage to your insurance to your car loan. It deserves your attention. Checking it before you buy is not optional. It's one of the most important steps in the whole process.

That's my story. I hope it helps you avoid the mistake I made. Check your score early, fix what you can, and walk into the lender's office knowing exactly what you're working with. Your future self will thank you.

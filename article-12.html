Why Home Inspections Matter

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

What a home inspection is

A home inspection is a visual examination of a home’s condition by a trained inspector. The inspector looks at the structure, roof, exterior, attic, plumbing, electrical system, heating and cooling, and the visible interior, then writes a report that lists defects and safety concerns. It is not a guarantee, and it is not an appraisal. An appraisal estimates value for the lender. An inspection tells you about condition for you.

What it covers and does not cover

  • Typically covered: roof condition, foundation and structure visible signs, grading and drainage, plumbing and electrical systems, water heater, HVAC, windows and doors, attic and insulation, and visible signs of moisture.
  • Often not covered: hidden problems behind walls, underground pipes, pests, mold testing, radon, asbestos, lead paint and detailed systems checks unless you add them.

Standards and licensing differ by state, so ask the inspector what the report includes.

Specialist add-ons

  • Sewer scope. A camera inspection of the line to the street.
  • Radon test. Measures a naturally occurring gas that can build up in homes.
  • Wood-destroying insect inspection. Checks for termites and similar damage.
  • Roof, chimney and structural specialists. When the general inspector sees signs of trouble.
  • Well and septic tests for homes with private systems.

Costs vary by region and home. General inspections commonly cost a few hundred dollars, and specialist tests add to that. Ask for quotes and decide which are worth it based on the home’s age, location and any warning signs.

Why it matters financially

Major systems such as the roof, foundation, heating and cooling or plumbing can cost thousands of dollars or more to repair or replace. Finding problems before you buy lets you ask for repairs, a credit or a lower price, or walk away if your contract allows. Even when nothing is wrong, the report gives you a maintenance list and helps you plan for the first year. A common rule of thumb is to set aside 1% to 2% of the home’s value each year for upkeep. On a $350,000 home that is roughly $3,500 to $7,000.

How to use the report

  • Separate safety issues and major defects from routine maintenance.
  • Ask your agent which items are reasonable to request and which are normal wear.
  • Get contractor estimates for the big items.
  • Decide whether to ask for repairs, a credit at closing or a price reduction.
  • Put agreed repairs in writing and verify them before closing.

A price reduction also lowers your loan. A $8,000 reduction on a $350,000 home with 20% down lowers the down payment by $1,600 and the payment by about $40 a month at 6.5%. A credit may help with closing costs but does not change the price.

The inspection contingency

Most purchase contracts include an inspection period, often a week or two, during which you can inspect and then request repairs, renegotiate or cancel without losing your deposit. The period has a deadline and the contract specifies how to respond. Missing it can mean you lose the right to object. Read the dates carefully and schedule the inspection immediately after your offer is accepted.

Choosing an inspector

  • Ask for their license or certification, if your state requires it, and professional association membership.
  • Read sample reports and check reviews.
  • Confirm insurance and how long the inspection takes.
  • Choose someone independent of the seller.
  • Attend the inspection and ask questions.

Your agent can suggest names, but you are free to choose.

If you are tempted to waive

In a competitive market buyers sometimes waive the inspection to strengthen an offer. That shifts all the risk of hidden defects onto you. Options that keep some protection include a shorter inspection period, inspecting before you make an offer if the seller allows it, limiting repair requests to major issues, or setting a repair-cost threshold. Always review your reserves before waiving, and read our bidding war guide.

New homes and condominiums

New construction can have defects too, so an independent inspection before closing is still worthwhile. For a condominium, the inspector examines the unit, while the association’s reserves, rules and building condition are separate questions. Ask for the association’s financial statements and recent meeting notes.

After you buy

Keep the inspection report. It identifies items to watch and schedule. Start a maintenance calendar for filters, gutters, water heater service and similar tasks, and set aside money regularly so repairs do not become emergencies.

A short checklist

  • Schedule the inspection right after the offer is accepted.
  • Decide which specialist tests are worthwhile.
  • Attend and ask questions.
  • Prioritize safety and major defects.
  • Get written repair agreements.
  • Keep reserves for the first year, as described in our first-time buyer guide.

What happens on inspection day

Plan to spend two to three hours or more at the home, depending on its size. The inspector walks the property, tests systems and takes photos. Attending lets you ask questions and see problems in person, which a written report cannot always convey. Ask the inspector to show you main shutoffs for water, gas and electricity, and to explain which issues are urgent. Most inspectors send the report within a day or two.

Reading the report

Reports can look alarming because they list everything the inspector noticed. Sort the findings into three groups to keep perspective.

CategoryExamplesWhat to do
Safety or major defectActive leaks, electrical hazards, structural movement, failing roofGet specialist estimates; negotiate or reconsider
Needs repair soonAging water heater, worn gutters, minor plumbing leaksBudget or request a credit
Routine maintenanceCaulking, filters, minor cosmetic itemsAdd to your maintenance list

Focus negotiation on the first two rows. Sellers often resist long lists of small items.

Warning signs that justify a specialist

  • Diagonal or stair-step cracks in walls or foundation, or doors and floors that are noticeably out of level.
  • Water stains, musty smells or signs of past leaks.
  • Older electrical systems such as knob-and-tube wiring or aluminum branch wiring.
  • Old plumbing materials that are prone to failure.
  • A roof or heating system at or near the end of its expected life.
  • Evidence of insects or animals.

A specialist can estimate the cost to fix a problem so you can negotiate with real numbers.

Negotiation options

  • Repairs by the seller. You control less over quality. Ask for receipts and permits.
  • Credit at closing. Often easier for sellers, but lender limits may apply, and you manage the repair.
  • Price reduction. Lowers your loan and down payment.
  • Walk away. If your contract allows it and the problems are too large.

Ask your agent which approach works best locally and get any agreement in writing before the inspection period ends.

Questions for the seller

Review the seller’s disclosure forms if your state provides them. Ask about past repairs, leaks, insurance claims, the age of the roof and major systems, and any permits for renovations. Keep the answers in writing. A mismatch between the disclosure and the inspection report is worth investigating.

Final thoughts

An inspection costs a small fraction of the price of a home and can save you far more than it costs. It will not find everything and cannot guarantee the home’s future, but it turns unknowns into numbers you can plan for. Treat it as part of the price you pay to buy confidently.

Plan for your first year of ownership

Even a clean inspection does not remove the need for reserves. Use the report to build a first-year plan: list the items inspectors flagged as aging, estimate their cost with a few contractor quotes and set aside money each month for them. Many owners keep a dedicated home-repair savings account separate from their emergency fund. If the report shows that big systems are near the end of their life, factor their replacement into your budget when you decide how much house you can afford (see our affordability guide). Buying with open eyes is the best protection against costly surprises.

Common questions

  • Who pays for the inspection? Normally the buyer, and it is paid when the inspection is done.
  • Can I inspect after I am under contract? Yes, within the contract’s inspection period.
  • Should I attend? Yes, if you can. You will learn more than the report shows.
  • Does the lender need it? Usually no. The lender orders an appraisal, which is different.
  • Can a seller refuse repairs? Yes. You then choose whether to proceed, renegotiate or cancel under your contract.

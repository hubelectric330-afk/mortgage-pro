The Danger of Waiving a Home Inspection

By A Regular Homebuyer | Updated November 2026 | 12 Min Read

I want to start this story by telling you what our house looked like the day we moved in. It was beautiful. Fresh paint, new carpets, a kitchen that looked like it belonged in a magazine. Our friends came over and told us how lucky we were. They said we had won the house lottery. And for about six months, I believed them. We had won a bidding war against five other offers, and we had done it by waiving the home inspection. Our agent told us it was the move that sealed the deal. She was right, and that was the problem.

Six months later, our basement flooded. Not a little. Not a trickle. A full, ankle-deep flood that ruined the carpet, soaked through the drywall, and destroyed about $4,000 worth of things we had stored down there. When we called a plumber, he took one look at the pipes and said, "This has been leaking slowly for years. Whoever inspected this house should have caught it." Except nobody had inspected the house, because we had waived the inspection. And that plumber's visit was the first of many that would cost us tens of thousands of dollars over the next two years.

I'm writing this because I don't want anyone else to make the same mistake we did. Waiving a home inspection feels like a smart competitive move in a hot market. It feels like a way to win. But what we learned, the hard way, is that waiving the inspection doesn't just save you the $500 inspection fee. It can cost you tens of thousands of dollars in hidden problems that you'll be paying for long after the bidding war is over.

Why We Waived the Inspection in the First Place

I want to be fair to my past self, and to anyone reading this who is thinking about doing the same thing. The pressure in a hot market is real. We had lost three houses before we found this one. Every single time, we were beaten by someone who offered more or waived more contingencies. Our agent told us that in this market, sellers weren't even looking at offers with inspection contingencies. They were picking the cleanest, safest offers, and a waived inspection was the way to signal that our offer was clean.

We were exhausted. We had been house hunting for eight months. We had a toddler and a second baby on the way. We were living in a cramped apartment, and every passing month felt like a wasted month. When our agent told us that waiving the inspection would give us a real shot, we said yes. We told ourselves that the house looked great. The seller had renovated it. The listing photos were beautiful. What could possibly be wrong?

The house looking great is not the same as the house being sound. Paint can hide water damage. New carpet can hide a rotting subfloor. A fresh coat of white paint on the basement walls can hide mold. The things that look expensive to fix are exactly the things that can be covered up with a weekend of cosmetic work.

What We Found in the First Year

I'm going to walk you through the problems we discovered, not to scare you, but to show you what waiving the inspection can actually cost. Every single one of these problems would have been caught by a competent home inspector, and every single one of them would have given us leverage to negotiate the price, ask the seller to fix something, or walk away entirely.

1. The basement leak.

This was the big one. The basement had a slow leak in a pipe behind the wall that had been going on for years. The seller had painted over the water stains and put new drywall in front of the pipes. An inspector would have run the water, checked the pipes, and probably spotted the moisture. We didn't. Total cost to fix and repair the damage: about $8,500.

2. The roof.

About nine months in, we noticed a stain on the ceiling in the upstairs hallway. A roofer told us the shingles were at the end of their life and had probably been patched before the sale. The seller had clearly put off replacing the roof and let it become our problem. A roof replacement on our house cost us $12,000.

3. The HVAC system.

The air conditioning worked fine the day we moved in. It stopped working two summers later. The HVAC technician told us the unit was over twenty years old, well past its expected life. A home inspector would have noted the age of the system and warned us that it was due for replacement. We had no idea. Total cost to replace: $7,200.

4. The electrical panel.

This one was the scariest. We had an electrician come out to install a new outlet, and he told us the electrical panel was outdated and didn't meet current safety standards. He said it was a fire risk. We had to replace the entire panel, which cost $3,500. An inspector would have flagged this immediately, and we could have asked the seller to fix it or walked away.

Here's the truth that nobody told us: A home inspection typically costs $400 to $700. The problems we found in our first two years cost us over $31,000. Every single one of them would have been caught in a two-hour inspection. We saved $500 upfront and lost $31,000 on the back end. That is the real math of waiving an inspection.

What an Inspection Actually Does

Before we went through all of this, I thought a home inspection was mostly about finding small problems so you could ask the seller to fix them. I was wrong. A good home inspector is a trained eye who walks through the house and looks for the things that a buyer can't see. They check the roof, the foundation, the plumbing, the electrical system, the HVAC, the attic, the basement, and the crawl spaces. They run the water, test the outlets, look for signs of moisture, and check for structural issues. They take hours to do this. They know what to look for because they've seen hundreds of houses.

An inspection isn't a guarantee. It doesn't catch everything. But it catches the big things, the expensive things, the things that will cost you tens of thousands of dollars after you move in. And even if you don't plan to ask the seller to fix anything, the inspection gives you information. It tells you what you're really buying. It lets you make an informed decision instead of a hopeful one.

What We Would Have Done Differently

Looking back, there were a few things we could have done that would have let us stay competitive without waiving the inspection entirely. Here's what I wish we had known.

1. An "information only" inspection.

This is a common compromise in hot markets. You do the inspection for your own information, but you agree not to ask the seller for repairs and not to walk away based on what the inspection finds. The seller gets the certainty of a clean deal, and you get the information you need to understand what you're buying. It's not as good as a full inspection contingency, but it's much better than no inspection at all.

2. A pre-offer walkthrough with an inspector.

Some inspectors offer a short "walk-and-talk" before you make an offer. You pay a smaller fee, and the inspector walks through the house with you and points out obvious red flags. It's not a full inspection, but it can catch things you'd miss and help you decide whether to make an offer at all.

3. Bring a contractor friend.

This one saved my brother when he bought his house. He brought a contractor friend to the open house, and the friend spotted signs of water damage in the attic that the seller had painted over. He didn't waive the inspection, but even if he had, he would have known what he was walking into.

4. Negotiate the price instead of the inspection.

In a hot market, offering a little more money and keeping the inspection contingency can be a better deal than offering less and waiving the inspection. The extra money you offer is a known cost. The cost of a hidden problem is not. Don't trade a small known cost for a large unknown risk.

The inspection contingency is not a weakness. It's a safety net. In a competitive market, sellers may prefer offers without it, but that doesn't mean you should give it up. There are ways to stay competitive without removing the one protection that could save you tens of thousands of dollars.

What the First Two Years Actually Cost Us

I want to put the numbers in front of you, because this is the part that made the biggest impression on me. Here's what we spent on repairs in our first two years, compared to the $500 we would have spent on an inspection.

Problem Cost Would Inspection Have Caught It?
Basement leak and repairs $8,500 Almost certainly
Roof replacement $12,000 Yes
HVAC replacement $7,200 Yes
Electrical panel replacement $3,500 Yes
Total Repairs ~$31,200 All of them
Cost of an Inspection $500 N/A

We saved $500 and spent $31,200. That's not a smart financial move. That's a very expensive mistake. And the worst part is, we would have known. We could have negotiated. We could have walked away. We could have asked the seller to fix some of it or reduce the price. Instead, we found out after the fact, with no leverage and no recourse.

What I Want You to Take From This

I know how tempting it is to waive the inspection. I've been there. I've felt the exhaustion of losing offers and the desperation to finally win. But I want you to remember what we learned. Waiving the inspection is not a small risk. It's a big one. It's a risk that could cost you a year's salary, or more, in a house you're supposed to be excited to live in.

If you're in a competitive market, there are ways to stay in the game without giving up the inspection entirely. Do an information-only inspection. Do a pre-offer walkthrough. Bring someone knowledgeable with you. Talk to your agent about strategies that keep some protection in place. And if you absolutely have to waive the inspection to win the house, go in with your eyes open. Budget for the possibility of major repairs. Set aside an emergency fund that can cover the surprises. And know that what you're doing is a calculated risk, not a free win.

The house we bought was not a bargain. It was a lottery ticket we bought without knowing the odds. If I could go back, I would have done the inspection. I would have risked losing the house to gain the information. Because a house that costs you $31,000 in surprise repairs is not a house you won. It's a house that won you.

Today, our house is fine. We fixed everything, and we love living here. But we paid for that love in a way that we didn't have to. If you're buying a house right now, please don't make the same mistake. Pay for the inspection. Keep the contingency. Protect yourself. The right house will come along, and when it does, you'll be glad you didn't trade your peace of mind for a bidding war win.

That's my story. I hope it saves someone out there from the same surprise we had.

— A Regular Homebuyer (and SmartLoanCalc Reader)

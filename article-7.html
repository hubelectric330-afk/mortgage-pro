How Amortization Schedules Work

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

What amortization means

Amortization is the process of paying off a loan in scheduled installments. On a standard fixed-rate mortgage each payment is the same amount, but its makeup changes every month. Interest is charged on the remaining balance, so early payments carry the most interest. Whatever is left after interest reduces the principal. As the balance falls, interest shrinks and more of the same payment goes to principal.

The schedule is a table that lists every payment, how much was interest, how much was principal and the remaining balance. Lenders must disclose the loan terms, and most servicers provide a full schedule on request. The calculator shows a yearly version.

Month by month: how one payment is split

Take a $300,000 loan at 6.5% for 30 years. The monthly principal-and-interest payment is $1,896. In the first month the interest is balance × monthly rate, or $300,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $1,625. That leaves $271 for principal, so the new balance is $299,729. Next month interest is calculated on the lower balance, so the principal part grows by a little. Repeating this 360 times pays the loan off exactly.

Why this matters for equity

Early in a loan, almost all your gain in ownership comes from the down payment and from any rise in the home’s value, not from payments. If you sell after a few years you will still owe most of the loan, and selling costs such as agent commissions and closing fees can take a meaningful share of the sale price. This is one reason a short stay in a purchased home can cost more than renting. It is also why lenders and advisors suggest planning to stay for several years.

Extra payments and the schedule

Extra principal changes the schedule because it reduces the balance that future interest is charged on. In the $300,000 example, adding one extra monthly payment per year (the payment ×13 ÷ 12 each month) would pay the loan off in about 24 years and 2 months and cut total interest by roughly $87,256. Confirm there is no prepayment penalty and that the servicer applies the extra amount to principal. See our extra payment guide.

Things the schedule does not show

  • Escrow. Property tax and insurance collected with your payment are not part of the principal-and-interest schedule, and they can change. See escrow explained.
  • PMI. It is added to the payment until it ends (see our PMI guide).
  • Rate changes. Adjustable-rate loans recalculate the schedule when the rate resets, so the table you receive at closing only covers the introductory period.
  • Late fees and fees on the servicing side.

Interest-only and negative-amortization loans work differently and are uncommon for ordinary home loans. If a loan has either feature, make sure you understand how and when the balance will start to fall.

How to read your own schedule

  • Check that the first-month interest equals balance × annual rate ÷ 12.
  • Look at the balance after five and ten years to see how much equity payments have built.
  • Find the year principal passes interest.
  • Compare the total interest to the loan amount to see the cost of borrowing.
  • Compare the schedule to the Closing Disclosure and your first statement.

If the numbers do not match your statement, ask the servicer to explain the difference, which may be escrow, PMI or how payments are rounded.

Year 1 versus year 30

Compare the first and last payments on the same loan. Payment 1 is $1,896: $1,625 interest and $271 principal. Payment 120 is the same $1,896, but $1,380 is interest and $516 is principal. By payment 360 only about $10 is interest and $1,886 reduces the balance. The payment never changes; only its contents do. This is simply how interest on a falling balance works.

Is this a trick by lenders?

No. Interest is charged on what you owe, so when you owe more, you pay more interest. A level payment is chosen so that the loan is exactly repaid on schedule and so borrowers can budget the same amount every month. The consequence is that equity builds slowly at first. The practical lesson is to understand the cost of borrowing before you commit and to consider ways, such as extra principal, to reduce it.

Biweekly payments

Some lenders and third parties offer biweekly payment plans, where you pay half the monthly amount every two weeks. Because there are 26 half-payments in a year, you end up paying the equivalent of 13 monthly payments instead of 12. On the $300,000 example that would shorten a 30-year loan to about 24 years and 2 months, but only because of the extra payment, not because of the schedule. You can get the same result by adding one-twelfth of a payment each month. Watch for fees on third-party biweekly programs and confirm that your servicer applies payments to principal promptly.

Recasting and refinancing

  • Recasting. After a large principal payment, some lenders will re-amortize the loan on the lower balance, keeping the rate and term but lowering the payment. It usually carries a small fee. Not all lenders offer it.
  • Refinancing. Replaces the loan with a new one, resetting the schedule. It can cut the rate but restarts the early, interest-heavy phase unless you pick a shorter term. See our refinancing guide.

Questions to ask your servicer

  • Can I see the full amortization schedule?
  • How do you apply extra payments, and how do I direct them to principal?
  • Is there a prepayment penalty?
  • Do you offer recasting, and what does it cost?
  • How is my escrow account separate from the schedule?

A worked reading exercise

Try this with any loan. First, multiply the balance by the annual rate and divide by 12 to find the month’s interest. Second, subtract that from the payment to find the principal. Third, subtract the principal from the balance to get the new balance. On the example loan, month one gives $1,625 interest, $271 principal and a new balance of $299,729. If your servicer’s statement shows different numbers, check whether the payment includes escrow or PMI, whether the loan has an adjustable rate, and whether any extra payment was applied.

Why the early years matter most for savings

Because interest is charged on the largest balance in the early years, a dollar of extra principal paid early prevents interest on that dollar for the longest time. A dollar paid in year 25 saves only a few years of interest. That is why people who plan to prepay usually start sooner rather than later, and why refinancing late in a loan into a new 30-year term can raise total interest even if the rate is lower. When comparing options, look at total interest and the payoff date, not only the monthly payment.

Putting it to use

  • Use the calculator to view your yearly schedule.
  • Decide whether you will stay long enough to benefit from extra payments.
  • Compare the cost of extra payments against other goals such as an emergency fund and retirement saving.
  • Review the schedule yearly, and after any large payment.

Understanding amortization turns a mortgage statement from a mystery into a table you can check.

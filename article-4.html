Closing Costs: What to Budget Beyond the Down Payment

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

What closing costs are

Closing costs are the fees and prepaid items you pay to complete a home purchase and loan, in addition to the down payment. They go to the lender, to third parties such as the appraiser and title company, to local governments, and into escrow accounts for future taxes and insurance. Buyers are often surprised by them because they are paid at closing rather than built into the price of the home. Knowing the likely amount early prevents a last-minute scramble for cash.

Sellers also pay costs, usually including agent commissions, but this guide covers what a buyer should expect.

How much to budget

Closing costs vary with the loan size, the location, the lender and the type of loan. A commonly used estimate is about 2% to 5% of the purchase price. Transfer taxes, title insurance rates and recording fees differ a great deal between states and even counties. The table shows the range for three example prices.

Purchase priceAt 2%At 3%At 5%
$250,000$5,000$7,500$12,500

These are estimates, not quotes. Your Loan Estimate itemizes the real figures for your loan.

What is included

  • Lender charges. Origination or underwriting fees, application and processing fees, and discount points if you choose them.
  • Appraisal and inspections. The lender orders an appraisal; you may separately pay for a home inspection and specialist reports.
  • Credit report and flood determination. Typically modest fees.
  • Title charges. Title search, lender’s title insurance (almost always required) and optional owner’s title insurance, plus settlement or attorney fees depending on the state.
  • Government fees. Recording fees and, in many places, transfer or mortgage taxes.
  • Prepaids. Interest from the closing date to the end of the month, a first-year homeowners insurance premium and sometimes several months of property tax.
  • Escrow deposits. An initial cushion for taxes and insurance if your loan has escrow. See escrow explained.

The Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure

Federal rules require your lender to provide a Loan Estimate within three business days after you apply. It lists the interest rate, monthly payment and itemized closing costs in a standard format, which makes it possible to compare lenders. You must receive the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing. Compare it line by line with the Loan Estimate.

Some fees are limited by rules: certain lender charges may not rise from the estimate at all, some may rise by no more than 10% in total, and others, such as prepaid insurance and some third-party services you were allowed to shop for, may change. If a figure jumps, ask for an explanation in writing before closing day.

Discount points and lender credits

A discount point is a fee equal to 1% of the loan amount paid at closing in exchange for a lower rate. Lender credits work the other way: you accept a higher rate and the lender covers some closing costs. Suppose a point on a $280,000 loan costs $2,800 and cuts the rate from 6.5% to a hypothetical 6.25%. The monthly principal-and-interest payment falls from $1,770 to $1,724, a saving of about $46 a month. The break-even point is about 61 months, or 5.1 years. If you will keep the loan and home longer than that, paying points may save money; if you may sell or refinance sooner, credits can be cheaper. Compare quotes on the Loan Estimate.

Ways to reduce closing costs

  • Get Loan Estimates from several lenders and compare the “Origination Charges” and total closing costs, not just the rate.
  • Shop the services you are allowed to shop for, such as title insurance and settlement services.
  • Ask the seller to contribute toward your costs. Limits depend on the loan type and down payment.
  • Ask about lender credits and compare against a higher rate.
  • Ask about state or local assistance programs.
  • Time your closing near the end of the month to reduce prepaid interest.

Negotiating is normal. Lenders can sometimes waive or reduce some fees, particularly when you have competing offers.

Planning your cash to close

Add the down payment, closing costs and a reserve for the first months. The table shows one hypothetical plan for a $350,000 home with 20% down, estimating closing costs at 3% and moving and repairs at $4,000.

Avoiding mistakes

  • Do not assume the down payment is all the cash you need.
  • Do not move money between accounts without a paper trail before closing. Lenders may ask you to explain large deposits.
  • Confirm wire instructions by phone using a number you already know. Fake instructions are a common scam.
  • Do not make large purchases or open new credit before closing.
  • Read the Closing Disclosure carefully.

A short checklist

  • Estimate 2% to 5% of the price and check it against your Loan Estimate.
  • Compare at least three lenders.
  • Ask about points, credits and seller concessions.
  • Review the Closing Disclosure three business days before closing.
  • Confirm the final cash-to-close amount and how to send it.
  • Keep an emergency reserve after closing.

Comparing two lender offers

Imagine Lender A offers 6.5% on a $280,000 loan with $4,200 of lender charges, and Lender B offers 6.375% with $6,100. Lender B’s payment is lower by about $23 a month, but you pay $1,900 more upfront. The table adds charges and principal-and-interest payments over different time frames. Closing costs by loan type

  • Conventional loans. Costs follow the standard categories; PMI may be added if the down payment is under 20%.
  • FHA loans. Include an upfront mortgage insurance premium, which many borrowers add to the loan.
  • VA loans. Most borrowers pay a funding fee unless exempt; some closing costs are limited.
  • USDA loans. Include an upfront guarantee fee that can often be financed.

Programs and fees change, so confirm current figures with a lender or the program’s official website.

“No-closing-cost” loans

Some lenders advertise loans with no closing costs. The costs are not gone: they are either folded into a higher interest rate or added to the loan balance. These loans can make sense if you expect to move or refinance soon and want to preserve cash. If you plan to keep the loan for many years, the higher rate usually costs more in total. Ask for the Loan Estimate for both versions and compare the total cost over the time you expect to hold the loan.

Common questions

  • Can I finance closing costs? Sometimes, through a higher rate, lender credit or a larger loan in some programs, but it raises long-term cost.
  • Are closing costs tax deductible? Some items, such as points, may have tax treatment. Ask a tax professional.
  • Do refinances have closing costs too? Yes, often similar in size. See our refinancing guide.
  • Why do costs vary by state? Taxes, title rules and required services differ.
  • Can I back out if the costs jump? Depending on your contract and timing, you may have options, so ask your agent and lender before the deadline.

Putting it together

Closing costs are a real part of the price of a home, but they are also one of the more negotiable parts. Estimate them early, compare lender offers on the same day, read both the Loan Estimate and the Closing Disclosure carefully, and keep a cash cushion after closing. A buyer who knows the numbers in advance can walk into closing calmly instead of facing a surprise bill.

Refinancing: When It Can and Cannot Save Money

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

What refinancing is

Refinancing replaces your current mortgage with a new one, usually to get a lower rate, change the term, switch between fixed and adjustable rates, remove mortgage insurance, or take cash out of your home equity. The new loan pays off the old one and starts its own schedule and closing costs. Because of those costs, a refinance only helps if the benefits last long enough to outweigh them.

Main types

  • Rate-and-term refinance. Changes the rate, the term or both, without taking cash out.
  • Cash-out refinance. A larger new loan lets you take some equity in cash. The home secures the larger debt.
  • Streamline refinances. Some government-backed loans, such as FHA and VA programs, offer simplified refinances with less documentation. Eligibility rules apply.

Costs

Refinance closing costs are often similar to a purchase loan, commonly estimated at roughly 2% to 6% of the loan amount depending on the lender, location and loan size. They can include origination fees, appraisal, title insurance, recording fees and prepaid items. Your Loan Estimate lists them. You can pay in cash, roll them into the loan, or accept a higher rate in return for a lender credit. Rolling costs into the loan raises the balance and the interest you pay on it.

Break-even math

The core test is simple: break-even months equal closing costs divided by monthly savings. Take a $280,000 balance at 7.25% with 27 years remaining, with a payment of about $1,972. Refinancing to 6.25% over 30 years lowers the payment to about $1,724, a monthly saving of $248. With $6,500 of closing costs, break-even is about 26 months, or 2.2 years. If you keep the loan longer than that, the lower payment adds up to real savings; if you sell or refinance sooner, you lose money.

Beware the term reset

Part of that lower payment comes from stretching 27 remaining years back to 30. Compare total interest from today.

Refinancing to a shorter or equal remaining term keeps the savings honest. Some borrowers refinance into a new 30-year loan for the lower payment and then pay the old payment amount to keep the original payoff date. Check the figures for your own loan.

Cash-out refinancing

A cash-out refinance turns equity into cash but also turns it into debt. On a $280,000 balance, a $50,000 cash-out at a hypothetical 6.5% creates a $330,000 loan with a payment of about $2,086, compared with $1,770 on $280,000 at the same rate, an increase of about $316 a month. Cash-out loans often carry a slightly higher rate and stricter equity limits. Use them for purposes that add lasting value, and avoid funding short-term spending with a 30-year secured loan.

When refinancing can make sense

  • The rate drop is large enough that break-even is short. A widely quoted rule of thumb is a drop of about 0.5 to 1 percentage point, but the break-even calculation is better than any rule.
  • You are removing PMI after building equity (see our PMI guide).
  • You are moving from an adjustable rate to a fixed rate before a reset (see fixed vs ARM).
  • You can shorten the term without straining your budget.
  • You plan to stay in the home well past break-even.

When to be cautious

  • You may move within a few years.
  • The rate improvement is small.
  • You are close to the end of your loan.
  • The closing costs are rolled into a larger balance.
  • Your credit or home value has fallen, which can raise your new rate.

How lenders decide

Lenders review credit, income, existing debts and the home’s value. Loan-to-value ratio matters: borrowers with at least 20% equity generally get the best pricing and avoid PMI. An appraisal is often required, though some streamline programs waive it. Shop several lenders, since rates and fees vary, and compare Loan Estimates on the same day.

For refinances of a primary residence, federal rules generally give you three business days after signing to cancel (the right of rescission), so funds are typically released after that period.

Questions to ask

  • What is the total closing cost, and what is the lender credit or rate if I choose none?
  • How long will it take to break even?
  • Does the new loan reset the term?
  • Is there a prepayment penalty on the new loan?
  • Will my escrow account change?

A short checklist

  • List your current rate, balance and remaining term.
  • Get at least three Loan Estimates.
  • Compute break-even for each.
  • Compare total interest, not just the monthly payment.
  • Decide how long you will keep the loan.
  • Review the Closing Disclosure before signing.

Use the calculator to compare payments, and read how amortization works to see how a new loan restarts the schedule.

The refinance process, step by step

  • 1. Define the goal. Lower payment, shorter term, no PMI, fixed rate or cash. Your goal decides which loan to compare.
  • 2. Gather documents. Pay stubs, tax returns, bank statements, your current mortgage statement and homeowners insurance details.
  • 3. Compare lenders. Request Loan Estimates on the same day, with the same loan type and rate lock period.
  • 4. Lock the rate. A rate lock holds the quoted rate for a set number of days. Ask about the lock length and any fees for extending it.
  • 5. Appraisal and underwriting. The lender verifies your income, credit and the home’s value, which can take weeks.
  • 6. Review the Closing Disclosure. Compare it to your Loan Estimate at least three business days before closing.
  • 7. Close and confirm. After signing and any rescission period, confirm that your old loan is paid off and set up the new payment.

How equity and credit affect your rate

Lenders price refinances using your credit score and your loan-to-value ratio, which is the new loan divided by the home’s appraised value. Borrowers with strong credit and at least 20% equity generally receive the best pricing and avoid PMI. A lower score or less equity can raise the rate or add mortgage insurance, which can erase the benefit of refinancing. Before applying, check your credit reports and correct errors (see our credit score guide).

Example: refinancing to remove PMI

Suppose your home is now worth $330,000 and your balance is $255,000, a loan-to-value ratio of about 77%. If you pay PMI at a hypothetical 0.85% a year, that is about $181 a month. If a refinance costs $5,000 and removes the PMI without raising your rate, break-even is about 28 months. Before refinancing, check whether you can instead cancel PMI directly with your servicer, which usually costs much less (see our PMI guide).

Refinancing out of an adjustable rate

If you hold an ARM approaching its first adjustment, a fixed-rate refinance can remove the uncertainty. Start the process several months before the reset, because rates, home values and your credit can all change. Compare the new fixed payment with the capped ARM payment, and keep in mind that a refinance has closing costs. See our fixed vs ARM guide.

Watch for pressure and scams

Unsolicited offers by phone, mail or email promising guaranteed low rates, or asking for upfront fees, deserve caution. Check that a lender or loan officer is licensed, for example through the Nationwide Multistate Licensing System consumer access website, and never share account numbers or Social Security numbers with an unverified contact. Take your time and compare offers.

Final thoughts

A refinance is a tool, not an upgrade. The right time depends on how much you save, how long you will keep the loan, and what the new loan does to your total cost. Write down the numbers, compare at least three offers and decide using break-even and total interest. If the math does not clearly work, waiting costs nothing.

Run your own numbers

Before you call a lender, write down your balance, rate, remaining term and monthly payment. Ask each lender for a Loan Estimate and fill in the same table for each offer: new payment, closing costs, break-even months and total interest from today. Seeing the offers side by side makes the best choice obvious and protects you from sales pressure. Revisit the numbers if rates move, because a refinance that does not pay today may work later, and one that works today may stop working if your plans change.

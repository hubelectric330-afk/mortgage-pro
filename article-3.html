PMI: What It Costs and How to Remove It

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

What PMI is and who it protects

Private mortgage insurance, or PMI, is insurance that covers the lender, not you, if you stop paying a conventional loan. Lenders usually require it when your down payment is less than 20% of the home’s value, because a smaller down payment leaves them with less cushion if the home must be sold for less than the loan balance. You pay the premium, but the policy protects the lender. It does not protect you from foreclosure and does not pay your mortgage if you lose income.

PMI is different from homeowners insurance, which covers damage to the property, and from mortgage life or disability insurance, which are separate products. The charge usually appears on your Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure, so you can see it before you commit.

What PMI costs

PMI is quoted as a percentage of the original loan amount per year and is commonly paid monthly. Typical rates range from roughly 0.3% to over 1% a year, and some borrowers pay more. The table shows the monthly cost on a $285,000 loan, which is a $300,000 purchase with 5% down.

The final rate depends on your credit score, down payment, loan type and term, debt-to-income ratio and the insurer’s pricing. Borrowers with high scores and larger down payments pay the least. Because the price varies so much, compare PMI quotes between lenders rather than assuming an average.

How you can pay for PMI

  • Monthly premium. The most common choice. The cost is added to your payment and can be cancelled later.
  • Single (upfront) premium. You pay once at closing, which lowers the monthly payment. If you sell or refinance early, you generally do not get the money back.
  • Lender-paid PMI. The lender covers the premium in exchange for a higher interest rate. The rate increase stays for the life of the loan, even after you would have cancelled monthly PMI.
  • Split premium. A smaller upfront payment plus a smaller monthly charge.

Which is cheaper depends on how long you keep the loan. Ask each lender for side-by-side figures and compare the total cost over the time you expect to stay.

Comparing down payment sizes

A larger down payment lowers the loan, the payment and the PMI. The table compares three down payments on a $300,000 home at 6.5% over 30 years, using hypothetical PMI rates of 0.75% for 5% down, 0.5% for 10% down and none at 20%. Your real quotes will differ.

Waiting to save 20% avoids PMI but can also mean waiting while prices and rents change. Buying with less down gets you in sooner at a higher monthly cost. Neither is automatically right. What matters is whether the payment fits your budget and you still keep an emergency fund after paying the down payment and closing costs.

How to cancel PMI on a conventional loan

The federal Homeowners Protection Act gives conventional borrowers the right to ask for PMI cancellation when the balance reaches 80% of the home’s original value, and requires automatic termination when the scheduled balance reaches 78%, if you are current on payments. Cancellation at 80% is not automatic: you generally must request it in writing, have a good payment history, and show that the property’s value has not declined and that there are no junior liens. Lenders may require an appraisal.

On the $285,000 example at 6.5% over 30 years, the balance first falls to 80% of the $300,000 original value ($240,000) after about 10 years and 4 months of scheduled payments, and to 78% ($234,000) after about 11 years and 3 months. Extra principal payments can reach those thresholds sooner. If your home has risen in value, you can ask the servicer about cancellation based on a new appraisal, though many servicers require a minimum seasoning period and have their own rules.

FHA, VA and USDA loans are different

  • FHA loans charge a mortgage insurance premium (MIP), both upfront and annually. On loans with less than 10% down, MIP can last for the life of the loan, so refinancing into a conventional loan is a common way to remove it once you have enough equity.
  • VA loans do not require monthly mortgage insurance, but most borrowers pay a one-time funding fee unless exempt.
  • USDA loans charge a guarantee fee upfront and annually.

These rules change, so confirm current requirements with a lender or on the program’s official website.

Ways to avoid or reduce PMI

  • Save a 20% down payment, where practical.
  • Ask about lender-paid PMI and compare the higher rate against the PMI savings.
  • Improve your credit score before applying, since pricing drops at higher score tiers.
  • Ask whether a piggyback second mortgage is available. These can carry their own rates, risks and requirements, so compare total cost.
  • Ask your agent about down payment assistance programs in your area.

A short checklist

  • Find PMI on the Loan Estimate and compare it across lenders.
  • Know when the balance reaches 80% and 78% of the original value.
  • Ask the servicer how to request cancellation and what documents are required.
  • Consider extra principal payments to reach 80% sooner (see our extra payment guide).
  • Use the calculator to see the full payment with PMI included.

PMI is a cost of buying with less money down, not a penalty. The goal is to know the cost in advance, compare options and plan to remove it as soon as you are allowed to.

Is paying PMI worth it?

It depends on what the alternative is. Suppose you can buy now with 5% down and pay PMI at a hypothetical 0.75%, about $178 a month. If PMI stayed until the balance reached 80% of the original value on schedule, that would be roughly 124 payments, or about $22,088 in total. Against that, you would be a homeowner years earlier, building equity if prices hold and no longer paying rent. If you wait to save 20% instead, you avoid that cost but pay rent in the meantime and risk higher prices or rates. Neither choice is risk free. Run both with real numbers: your rent, your savings rate and local price trends.

Also consider the cash left after closing. A 20% down payment that leaves you with no emergency fund can be riskier than 5% down with six months of reserves.

Common questions

  • Does PMI end automatically? On conventional loans it must end when the scheduled balance reaches 78% of the original value if you are current, and you can request earlier removal at 80%. Terms vary by loan.
  • Can extra payments end it sooner? Yes, by lowering the balance faster, but you must still request cancellation at 80% and the lender may require an appraisal.
  • Does refinancing remove PMI? A new loan with at least 20% equity does not require PMI, but refinancing has closing costs, so compare the savings against those costs. See refinancing basics.
  • Is PMI tax deductible? Tax rules on mortgage insurance have changed over the years. Ask a tax professional about the current rules for your situation.
  • Why does my quote differ from my friend’s? Credit score, down payment, loan size, term and the insurer all affect pricing.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming PMI will disappear without a request. Confirm the process with your servicer.
  • Ignoring PMI when comparing lenders. A lower rate with higher PMI may cost more overall.
  • Choosing lender-paid PMI without comparing the higher rate over your expected time in the home.
  • Counting on home appreciation to cancel PMI. Servicers have rules about appraisals and how long you must have held the loan.
  • Letting the balance drift: track when you reach 80% so you can act promptly.

Keep PMI in perspective

PMI is one cost among many when you buy a home. Compare it with the cost of waiting, the size of your emergency fund, and the total payment you can carry. Ask lenders for a written PMI quote, note the date it can end, and set a reminder to request cancellation once your balance reaches 80% of the original value.

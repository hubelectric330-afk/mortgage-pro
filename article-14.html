What Happens at Closing

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

The timeline before closing

Closing is the final step where ownership transfers and the loan funds. In the days before, a few things must go smoothly. The lender completes underwriting and issues a clear-to-close. You must receive the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing. You confirm homeowners insurance, arrange your cash to close and do a final walk-through of the home. Any change to major terms, such as the loan product, can restart the three-day clock.

Reviewing your Closing Disclosure

The Closing Disclosure is a five-page form that lists your loan terms, projected payments and every cost. Compare it with your Loan Estimate and your contract. Look at the following carefully.

  • Loan amount, interest rate and loan term match what you were quoted.
  • Monthly payment, including whether the rate or balance can change.
  • Closing costs and any lender or seller credits.
  • Cash to close and where it should be sent.
  • Prepaid interest, taxes and insurance, which depend on your closing date.
  • Property address and names spelled correctly.

If anything differs, ask the lender to explain in writing before closing.

Estimating cash to close

Your final figure comes from the Closing Disclosure. This example is for a $350,000 home with 20% down.

ItemAmount
Down payment (20% of $350,000)$70,000
Closing costs (3% estimate)$10,500
Prepaid interest, tax and insurance deposits (included above)Included
Less earnest money already paid$-7,000
Less seller credit$-3,000
Estimated cash to close$70,500

The prepaid interest charged at closing covers the days between closing and the end of that month. On a $280,000 loan at 6.5%, interest is about $50 a day, so closing on the 15th (about 16 days left in a 30-day month) would add roughly $798. Closing near the end of the month reduces it, and your first payment date also changes.

The final walk-through

  • Check that agreed repairs are complete and that you have receipts or permits.
  • Test lights, outlets, faucets, appliances, heating and cooling.
  • Check that nothing was removed or damaged during the seller’s move.
  • Confirm the seller has left included items.
  • Report problems to your agent right away, before you sign.

What you sign

You will sign many documents, and most are standard. The main ones are the promissory note, which is your promise to repay, and the mortgage or deed of trust, which secures the loan with the property. You will also sign the Closing Disclosure and various title, tax and disclosure forms. The seller signs the deed that transfers ownership. Ask questions as you go, and do not feel rushed. Closing often takes an hour or two.

What to bring

  • Government-issued photo ID.
  • Proof of funds if required, or confirmation that a wire was sent.
  • Proof of homeowners insurance, if not already provided.
  • Your copy of the Closing Disclosure and contract.
  • A pen and any documents the title company requested.

Protect yourself from wire fraud

Criminals send fake emails that copy a title company or agent and give new wiring instructions. Always call the title company at a number you already know, not one in an email, to confirm instructions before sending money. Do not send money until you have verified. If you realize you sent funds incorrectly, contact your bank and the title company immediately.

Remote and electronic closings

Some closings use electronic signing or a mobile notary. The same steps apply: review the Closing Disclosure, verify funds and identity, and sign carefully. Ask what format your lender and state allow.

After closing

  • The deed and mortgage are recorded with the local government.
  • You receive keys and often copies of your documents. Keep copies safely.
  • Your first payment is usually due about one to two months after closing. The Closing Disclosure shows the date.
  • Your loan may be sold or transferred to a servicer. You should receive a notice. Confirm any changes before paying someone new.
  • Update your address, set up utilities and ask about homestead or other tax exemptions.

A purchase has no three-day right of rescission. That right applies to many refinances (see our refinancing guide).

A short checklist

  • Receive and review the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing.
  • Compare it with the Loan Estimate.
  • Arrange cash to close and verify wire details by phone.
  • Do the final walk-through.
  • Bring ID and documents.
  • Keep copies and set up your first payment.

Use the calculator to confirm your payment, and see our closing costs guide and escrow guide for the numbers in your Closing Disclosure.

Who is at the closing

The closing table usually includes you, a closing agent or attorney, and sometimes your real estate agent and the seller. Lenders do not always attend. The closing agent explains each document and collects signatures, and then makes sure funds are disbursed and documents recorded. In some states an attorney leads closings, in others a title or escrow company does. Ask who will run yours and what they charge.

Title insurance in plain terms

Title insurance protects against defects in a property’s ownership history, such as unpaid liens or filing errors. A lender’s policy protects the lender and is usually required. An owner’s policy protects you and is optional in many places, but it is generally worth considering, since a title problem can affect your ownership. Ask your title company what is covered and what it costs, since you can often shop for it (see our closing costs guide).

A ten-day countdown

  • 10 days: confirm your lender has everything and that the appraisal and title work are on track.
  • 7 days: arrange homeowners insurance and confirm the closing date and location.
  • 5 days: read the Closing Disclosure and compare it with your Loan Estimate.
  • 3 days: verify wire instructions by phone and arrange your funds.
  • 1 to 2 days: do the final walk-through, confirm your closing time and ID.

If something goes wrong

Closings can slip. A rate lock can expire, an appraisal can come in low, a document can be missing or funds can arrive late. If a delay threatens your lock or contract deadline, ask your lender and agent right away about extensions and their cost. If the Closing Disclosure shows an unexpected fee, ask for an explanation and a written correction. If you are unsure, you can delay signing to get answers. Do not sign something you do not understand.

Your first thirty days as an owner

  • Change the locks and learn where the main shutoffs are.
  • Set up utilities, internet and your address changes.
  • Put all closing documents in a safe place and make digital copies.
  • Note your first payment date and set up autopay if you wish.
  • Review the inspection report and start your maintenance calendar (see our inspection guide).
  • Check whether your area offers exemptions that reduce property tax.

Final thoughts

Closing day is mostly paperwork, and the real work happens before it. Read your Closing Disclosure early, verify instructions by phone, keep your financial picture unchanged, and ask questions as you sign. If everything matches what you were quoted, the day is simply a signature, and the keys are yours.

Questions to ask your closing agent

  • What is the total cash I need, and how and when should I send it?
  • Will you review each document with me before I sign?
  • Who holds my earnest money, and when is it applied?
  • When will the deed be recorded, and when will I get copies?
  • If there is a delay, what happens to my rate lock and contract dates?
  • Who do I call if I notice an error after closing?

A good closing agent welcomes these questions, and asking them early prevents last-minute surprises.

Common questions

  • Can I sign early or by mail? Sometimes, depending on the lender, title company and state. Ask what is allowed.
  • Can the closing be delayed? Yes, if a document is missing or numbers change. Ask how that affects your rate lock and contract.
  • What if the final figure is higher than expected? Ask for a line-by-line explanation and a corrected Closing Disclosure before you pay.
  • Do I get the keys at the table? Usually after funding and recording, which can be the same day or later depending on local practice.
  • Can someone sign for me? Only with a valid power of attorney that the lender and title company accept.

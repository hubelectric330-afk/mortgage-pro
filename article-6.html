How Much House Can You Afford? (Hint: Less Than the Bank Says)

By A Regular Homebuyer | Updated November 2026 | 12 Min Read

I still have the piece of paper. It's folded in a drawer in my kitchen, a little crumpled at the edges. On it, in big bold numbers, is the amount my bank said I could borrow: $480,000. I remember getting that pre-approval letter and feeling like I had just been handed a golden ticket. I called my mom. I texted my best friend. I started browsing listings in neighborhoods I had assumed were out of my reach. The bank said I could afford it. The bank was telling me I was ready.

A year later, looking back, that number terrifies me. If I had bought a house anywhere near $480,000, I would have been house-poor within months. I would have been one flat tire away from a very bad month. The bank's number was technically true — they would have happily lent me that money — but it had almost nothing to do with what I could actually afford. That's the gap nobody warns you about.

If you're holding a pre-approval letter right now and feeling excited, I get it. I was there. But before you fall in love with a house in that top range, let me walk you through what the bank doesn't count and what I learned the hard way.

What the Bank Actually Looks At

When a lender decides how much they'll lend you, they run your numbers through a formula. The biggest factor is your debt-to-income ratio, which is exactly what it sounds like — a comparison of your monthly debt payments to your monthly income. Most lenders want your total housing payment to be under 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total debts to be under 36% to 43%. Some loans stretch that even further.

Here's the part that took me a while to understand. The bank isn't asking "Can this person comfortably afford this house?" They're asking "What's the maximum this person could pay where we still feel reasonably safe?" Those are two very different questions. The bank is managing risk, not your life. If you lose your job or your car breaks down or your kid needs braces, that's your problem. The bank just wants to make sure they get paid first.

The pre-approval number is not a budget. It's a ceiling. And in my experience, the ceiling and the comfortable life underneath it are usually thousands of dollars apart.

The Number That Changed Everything

Before I went house hunting, I did something that felt a little boring at the time. I sat down with my actual bank statements, not a spreadsheet I built from memory, and I tracked every dollar I spent for two months. I wanted to know what my real life cost. Not what I wished it cost — what it actually cost.

What I found was humbling. Between groceries, gas, insurance, phone bills, subscriptions I forgot I had, my gym membership, and the money I spent on coffee and takeout because I was tired, my real monthly spending was about $2,700. That was before I added a mortgage, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and utilities on top of it.

When I ran the numbers on that $480,000 loan at the going rate, my monthly payment would have been around $2,900 before taxes and insurance. Add property taxes and insurance, and I'd have been at $3,600 or more every month. That would have eaten almost everything I made. I wouldn't have been able to save. I wouldn't have been able to fix anything that broke. One surprise, and I'd be in trouble. The bank thought I was fine. I was not fine.

The Four Expenses the Bank Doesn't Count

Here are the things I learned to factor in that the bank never put on their form:

1. The maintenance reality.

When you own a home, things break. The water heater goes. The roof gets old. The dishwasher dies on a Sunday night. A good rule of thumb I've heard from other homeowners is to budget about 1% of your home's value per year for maintenance. On a $400,000 house, that's $4,000 a year — over $300 a month. The bank doesn't count this. But your house does.

2. Utilities you weren't paying before.

If you've been renting, there are costs you might not be thinking about. A bigger house means bigger heating and cooling bills. You might be paying for water, sewer, and trash when your landlord used to cover them. When I added up what a larger home would cost me in utilities, it was another $250 to $400 a month, depending on the season.

3. The life you actually want to live.

This is the one nobody talks about. The bank doesn't know you want to go on vacation. They don't know your kid plays travel soccer. They don't know you like to eat at a restaurant on your anniversary. They don't know you want to save for retirement or help your parents someday. All of that has to come from the money left over after your mortgage payment. If the mortgage eats it all, you've bought a house and given up your life.

4. The hidden costs of moving.

Moving itself costs money. Boxes, a truck, maybe movers, deposits on utilities, new furniture because the old couch doesn't fit the new living room. None of this is on the bank's radar, but all of it hits your wallet in the first few months of owning a home.

How I Found My Real Number

Once I understood what the bank was and wasn't counting, I did a simple exercise. I took my monthly take-home pay and subtracted every expense I already had that wasn't going away. Car payment, insurance, phone, groceries, gas, savings, subscriptions, everything. What was left over was the maximum I could put toward a house every month without changing my life at all.

That number was $2,100. Not $2,900. Not $3,600. $2,100. I went back to my lender and asked what price range that translated to, and after factoring in taxes and insurance, it put me at a house around $300,000 to $320,000. Nearly $160,000 less than what the bank had said I could afford.

I was disappointed at first. I had already started picturing myself in a bigger house. But then something clicked. If I bought at my real number, I would have room to breathe. I could still save. I could still take a vacation. I could handle a surprise without panicking. That feeling of safety was worth more than the extra square footage.

The Comparison That Opened My Eyes

Here's what the difference actually looked like in real numbers. I built this table to remind myself why I made the choice I did.

What Bank's Number ($480k) My Real Number ($310k)
Monthly Mortgage Payment $2,900 $1,870
Plus Taxes & Insurance +$700 +$450
Total Monthly Housing Cost ~$3,600 ~$2,320
Monthly Left for Life & Savings ~$400 ~$1,680
Room for a Surprise Repair? Barely Yes
Could Still Save for Retirement? Barely Comfortably

Looking at it in black and white, the choice became obvious. The bigger house would have looked impressive to my friends. The smaller house lets me sleep at night.

The trap to avoid: Lenders will often approve you for the absolute maximum they think you can handle. Some buyers take that as permission to go to the top of the range. Don't. The pre-approval number is a limit, not a recommendation. Treat it like the speed limit — you can go there, but you don't have to, and going 100% of the limit every time is how people crash.

What I'd Tell Anyone Getting Pre-Approved Today

If you're standing where I was, excited and a little overwhelmed, here's my honest advice. Take the pre-approval letter, thank your lender, and then set it aside. Don't start house hunting with that number in your head. Do what I did instead: track your real spending, figure out what a house payment would actually leave you with, and decide how much of your life you're willing to trade for a mortgage.

A house is a big decision, and I know it's tempting to stretch for the one that feels like the "right" house. But the right house isn't the biggest one you can qualify for. It's the one you can afford to live in comfortably for years, through the good months and the bad ones. The one that still leaves room for a vacation, a birthday dinner, an emergency fund, and a little breathing room at the end of the month.

The bank's number tells you what you can borrow. Your real number tells you what you can live with. Listen to the second one. It's the one that will keep you out of trouble five years from now.

I bought the $310,000 house. It's not the biggest one on the block. It's not the one my friends would have oohed and aahed over. But when I walk into my kitchen on a Sunday morning, I don't think about the mortgage. I think about the fact that I can afford my life and my house. That feeling is worth more than the extra square footage I left on the table.

If you take anything from this, let it be this: get the pre-approval, understand the number, and then decide for yourself what you can actually afford. Your bank doesn't know your life. Only you do.

— A Regular Homebuyer (and SmartLoanCalc Reader)

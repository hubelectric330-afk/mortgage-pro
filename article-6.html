Fixed-Rate vs Adjustable-Rate Mortgages

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

How a fixed-rate mortgage works

On a fixed-rate loan the interest rate stays the same for the entire term, so the principal-and-interest payment never changes. Your total payment can still change if taxes, insurance or PMI change, but the loan’s core cost is predictable. That certainty is the main reason most borrowers choose a 30-year fixed loan, particularly if they plan to stay in the home for many years.

How an adjustable-rate mortgage works

An adjustable-rate mortgage, or ARM, starts with a fixed introductory rate for a set period, then adjusts at regular intervals. The new rate equals an index plus a margin. The index follows a market benchmark, and many ARMs now use SOFR; the margin is set by the lender in your loan documents. Names such as 5/6 or 7/6 mean the rate is fixed for five or seven years and then adjusts every six months. A 10/6 ARM is fixed for ten years.

The introductory rate is usually lower than a comparable fixed rate, which is the reason people choose ARMs. The risk is that the rate may rise after the fixed period.

Rate caps

ARMs have caps that limit how far the rate can move. There is usually a cap on the first adjustment, a cap on each later adjustment and a cap on total increases over the life of the loan. A structure such as 2/2/5 means the rate can rise by at most 2 points at the first adjustment, 2 points at each later one and 5 points above the starting rate overall. Some loans have different caps, such as 5/1/5, so read the Loan Estimate. Caps limit the increase but do not remove it.

A worked example

Take a $300,000 loan. Compare a 30-year fixed loan at 6.5% with a 5/6 ARM starting at 6.0% and 2/2/5 caps (illustrative numbers). After 60 payments the ARM balance is about $279,163, and the payment after the first adjustment is recalculated over the remaining 25 years.

During the first five years the ARM saves about $98 a month, or roughly $5,853 in total. If the rate rises by 2 points, the payment goes up by about $258 a month more than the fixed payment, which would erase the early savings in about 23 months. If the rate falls, the ARM is cheaper. If it reaches the 11% cap, the payment could exceed $2,736.

When an ARM may make sense

  • You expect to sell or move before the fixed period ends.
  • You could comfortably afford the worst-case payment under the caps.
  • You have a plan and enough reserves if you cannot refinance when you want.
  • The initial savings are large enough to justify the risk.

Refinancing out of an ARM is never guaranteed. Rates may be higher, your home value or credit may have changed, and closing costs apply. Treat it as a possibility, not a plan.

When fixed is simpler

  • You plan to stay for many years.
  • Your budget is tight and cannot absorb a payment increase.
  • Your income is variable or uncertain.
  • You value being able to predict your costs.

Questions to ask a lender

  • What are the initial, periodic and lifetime caps?
  • What index and margin apply, and what would the payment be at the cap?
  • How long is the fixed period, and how often does the rate adjust after that?
  • Is there a prepayment penalty?
  • What advance notice will I get before a change?
  • Can the loan convert to a fixed rate, and at what cost?

Lenders must give written notice before the first adjustment and later changes, but notice does not stop the payment from rising.

Avoid these mistakes

  • Choosing an ARM because the first payment is lower without testing the capped payment.
  • Counting on a future refinance.
  • Ignoring the margin, which does not change.
  • Forgetting that the escrow part of your payment can rise even on a fixed loan.

A short checklist

  • Compare the fixed payment with the ARM start and capped payment.
  • Check your budget at the cap.
  • Compare the savings with the risk.
  • Read the loan terms and ask questions.
  • Use the calculator at both rates.

What happens at the first adjustment

About 60 months into a 5/6 ARM the lender recalculates. The new rate equals the index plus the margin, limited by the caps. Suppose the index is 4.5% and the margin is 2.75%. The fully indexed rate would be 7.25%. If your starting rate was 6.0% and the first adjustment cap is 2 points, the new rate can be at most 8.0%, so 7.25% applies. The payment is then recalculated on the remaining balance over the remaining term. The lender must tell you in writing in advance so you can plan or refinance.

Payment shock

Payment shock is the jump in payment when the rate adjusts. In the example, the payment rises from about $1,799 to about $2,155 even though the balance is lower, an increase of $356 a month, and the lifetime cap would allow more. Budget for the capped payment, not the starting one. If you could not pay it comfortably, an ARM adds a risk you may not want.

Managing ARM risk

  • Save the early payment difference instead of spending it.
  • Consider paying extra principal during the fixed period to lower the balance that will be recalculated (see our extra payment guide).
  • Keep a larger cash reserve.
  • Track rates and your credit so a refinance is easier.
  • Know the date of your first adjustment and calendar it.

Common questions

  • Can the payment ever fall? Yes, if the index drops, subject to any floors in your loan documents.
  • What is negative amortization? When payments are too small to cover interest, so the balance grows. Standard ARMs from mainstream lenders do not do this, but read the terms.
  • Is an ARM the same as an interest-only loan? No. Interest-only loans do not reduce principal for a period. They are separate products.
  • Do government loans offer ARMs? Some do, often with tighter caps, but terms vary.
  • Can I convert to fixed? Some loans allow it for a fee. Ask before you sign.

Choosing the fixed period

A longer introductory period gives more certainty but usually a smaller rate discount. A 5/6 ARM gives the largest discount and the shortest protection, a 7/6 sits in the middle, and a 10/6 behaves much like a fixed loan for a decade. Compare the discount with how long you realistically expect to hold the loan. If your plans are uncertain, a longer fixed period is more forgiving, because life events such as job changes, family growth or a move often arrive sooner or later than planned.

Putting it together

The choice between fixed and adjustable is a choice about which risk you prefer to carry. A fixed loan removes rate risk but costs more at the start. An ARM lowers early payments but leaves you exposed if rates rise and you cannot move or refinance. Run your own numbers in the calculator at the starting rate and at the capped rate, compare lenders on the same day, and choose the structure whose worst case you can live with. Whatever you choose, read the caps, the margin and the notice terms in the Loan Estimate before you sign, and keep enough savings to handle a higher payment if it comes.

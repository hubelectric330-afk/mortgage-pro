Common First-Time Buyer Mistakes

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

Why first-time buyers make costly mistakes

Buying a first home involves a large financial commitment, unfamiliar paperwork and a fast timeline. Most mistakes come from rushing, from budgeting only for the monthly payment, or from skipping steps that feel optional. Knowing the common pitfalls in advance lets you avoid most of them. The table shows a rough sense of what some of these mistakes can cost. The figures are examples, not predictions.

MistakeRough cost
Not comparing lenders (0.25-point rate difference on $280,000)$46 a month, about $16,482 over 30 years
Missing PMI in the budget (5% down, hypothetical 0.75% rate)about $178 a month
Underestimating closing costs (2% to 5% of $350,000)$7,000 to $17,500
No maintenance fund (1% to 2% of $350,000 a year)$3,500 to $7,000 a year
Selling within a few years (selling costs of 8% on $350,000, illustrative)$28,000

1. Not shopping for a lender

Many buyers take the first lender they talk to. Rates, fees and service differ, and the differences are real money. A quarter-point lower rate on a $280,000 loan saves about $46 a month. Request Loan Estimates from at least three lenders on the same day, with the same loan type and rate lock, and compare the interest rate, origination charges and total closing costs. Rate shopping within a short window generally counts as one inquiry on your credit. See our credit score guide.

2. Budgeting for the payment, not the house

A mortgage payment is only part of the cost of owning. Add property tax, homeowners insurance, PMI, HOA dues, utilities, maintenance and repairs. Make sure the total fits your take-home pay with room for savings and emergencies. Lenders may approve you for more than you can comfortably carry. Choose your own limit and test it at a higher rate. Our affordability guide explains how.

3. Skipping pre-approval

Pre-qualification is a quick estimate. Pre-approval involves documents and a credit check and tells you what a lender is likely to offer. Without it, you may fall in love with a home you cannot finance, and sellers may not take your offer seriously. Get pre-approved before you tour homes, and treat the amount as a ceiling, not a target.

4. Using all your savings for the down payment

A bigger down payment lowers your loan and may avoid PMI, but spending every dollar leaves you exposed. A broken water heater, a job interruption or a medical bill can happen right after you move in. Keep an emergency fund of several months of expenses after closing, in addition to the down payment, closing costs and moving expenses.

5. Underestimating closing costs

Closing costs are commonly about 2% to 5% of the price and are due at closing in addition to the down payment. Ask your lender for a Loan Estimate early and ask about seller credits and assistance programs. See our closing costs guide.

6. Skipping or waiving the inspection

An inspection can reveal expensive problems with the roof, foundation, wiring, plumbing or heating. Waiving it to win a bid shifts that risk to you. If you must compete, consider a shorter inspection period or limiting repair requests rather than giving up the inspection altogether. Read why inspections matter.

7. Ignoring PMI and loan terms

With less than 20% down, PMI can add a meaningful monthly cost, roughly $178 in the example, and you must plan to remove it (see our PMI guide). Also read for prepayment penalties, adjustable rates and balloon features. Make sure you understand whether a rate is fixed or adjustable (see fixed vs ARM).

8. Changing your finances before closing

  • Do not open new credit cards or finance a car or furniture.
  • Avoid big purchases on credit.
  • Keep paying every bill on time.
  • Do not move large sums between accounts without a record, since lenders may ask about deposits.
  • Avoid job changes or income changes without talking to your lender first.

The lender checks your credit and employment again before closing, and changes can derail the loan.

9. Letting emotion drive a bid

Competitive markets create pressure to overbid, waive protections and decide quickly. Set a price ceiling and walk-away number before you tour. Decide in advance how much appraisal gap, if any, you can cover. Read our bidding war guide.

10. Overlooking location costs

Property tax, insurance, HOA dues and commuting costs vary widely between neighborhoods. Two homes at the same price can have very different monthly costs. Ask for the actual tax bill and insurance quotes, including flood or wind coverage where relevant. Consider how long your commute is and what it costs.

11. Forgetting what selling costs

Buying and selling both have costs. Selling can include agent commissions, closing costs and moving expenses that often total several percent of the price, and sometimes close to 10%. If you may move within a few years, a purchase may not make financial sense compared with renting. Think about how long you plan to stay and run the numbers.

12. Not budgeting for maintenance

Homes need ongoing care. A common rule of thumb is to budget 1% to 2% of the home’s value each year for repairs and upkeep. Older homes may need more. Keep a separate repair fund so a surprise bill does not go on a credit card.

13. Not using the help available

  • HUD-approved housing counselors offer free or low-cost advice on budgeting and buying.
  • State and local housing agencies may offer down payment assistance or special loan programs for first-time buyers.
  • Lenders and loan officers can explain FHA, VA, USDA and conventional options.
  • Real estate attorneys or agents can explain contracts and contingencies.

Programs and eligibility change, so check official sources such as HUD and your state housing agency.

A short checklist

  • Check your credit reports and fix errors.
  • Get pre-approved after comparing lenders.
  • Decide your personal budget and price ceiling.
  • Save for the down payment, closing costs and an emergency fund.
  • Keep all contingencies you can afford to keep.
  • Do not change your finances before closing.
  • Plan for maintenance.

Use the calculator to test your payment, and read our guides on closing day and escrow before you sign.

A simple timeline for first-time buyers

  • 6 to 12 months out: check your credit reports, set your budget, start saving for the down payment, closing costs and reserves.
  • 3 to 6 months out: compare lenders, get pre-approved and ask about first-time buyer programs.
  • Searching: tour homes within your range, review comparable sales and keep your finances unchanged.
  • Under contract: schedule the inspection, review the Loan Estimate, lock your rate and arrange insurance.
  • Closing: review the Closing Disclosure, verify wire instructions by phone and complete the final walk-through.

Should you rent or buy?

Buying is not always better than renting. Owning adds costs that rent does not include, such as taxes, repairs and insurance, and it is harder to leave quickly. The longer you plan to stay, the more likely buying will pay off, because the large one-time costs of buying and selling are spread over more years. Compare your total monthly cost of owning with your rent, include maintenance, and consider how stable your job and plans are. If you may move in two or three years, renting may protect you from a loss.

Questions to ask before you make an offer

  • What is my total monthly cost including tax, insurance and maintenance?
  • How much cash will I have left after closing?
  • What happens if the appraisal comes in low?
  • What repairs are likely in the first few years?
  • How long do I realistically plan to stay?

Final thoughts

Most first-time buyer mistakes come down to the same few causes: rushing, budgeting too tightly and skipping protections. Take the time to compare lenders, set a personal limit, keep a reserve and read every document before you sign. A home bought with a clear plan is easier to enjoy, and it leaves room to handle the surprises that come with owning.

One more thing before you start

Write down your three biggest worries about buying, such as the payment, hidden repairs or the loan process, and match each with a step in this guide. Fear usually comes from not knowing the numbers. Once you know your budget, your reserves and your protections, the process becomes a series of manageable decisions, and you can move forward with confidence instead of guesswork.

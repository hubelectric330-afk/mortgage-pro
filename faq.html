The No-BS Mortgage FAQ

When I bought my first home, I felt like I was drowning in confusing jargon and hidden fees. I built this page to answer the questions I was too embarrassed to ask my lender. Real answers from a real homebuyer. No fluff, no spam.

2. Down Payments & PMI

Do I really need a 20% down payment?

No, you don't. This is one of the biggest myths in real estate. While 20% is ideal because it helps you avoid Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI), it is not a strict requirement. There are conventional loans that allow 3% down, FHA loans that allow 3.5% down, and if you are a veteran, VA loans might allow 0% down.

The key is to find a down payment amount that leaves you with enough savings for an emergency fund. Do not drain your life savings just to hit 20%. I've seen people buy a house with 20% down and then have zero dollars left to fix a broken furnace in the winter. That's a nightmare scenario. Keep your cash reserves.

What is PMI, and how do I get rid of it?

PMI stands for Private Mortgage Insurance. If you put down less than 20%, your lender sees you as a higher risk, so they require you to pay PMI. The ugly truth is that PMI protects the lender, not you. If you default on the loan, the insurance company pays the lender, not you.

The good news is that PMI doesn't last forever. Once you build up 20% equity in your home (either by paying down the loan or because the home value increased), you can request to have it removed. By law, the lender must automatically remove it once your balance reaches 78% of the original home value. When I reached that point, I called my lender immediately and got it removed, saving me $150 a month!

3. Rates, Lenders, and Credit

How do I compare lenders without getting scammed?

You need to get a "Loan Estimate" from at least three different lenders on the exact same day. This is crucial. Lenders can change rates daily. If you get an estimate from Bank A on Monday and Bank B on Friday, you aren't comparing apples to apples.

Look past the interest rate. Look at the "Origination Charges" (the fees the lender charges to make the loan) and the "Estimated Cash to Close." A lender might offer a slightly lower rate, but charge you $4,000 more in closing costs. Run the math to see if the lower rate actually saves you money in the long run.

Should I choose a 15-year or 30-year mortgage?

It depends entirely on your risk tolerance and budget. A 30-year mortgage has lower monthly payments, giving you more breathing room. A 15-year mortgage has higher monthly payments, but you pay off the house in half the time and save a massive amount of interest.

My personal strategy? I took out a 30-year mortgage for the safety of the lower payment, but I made extra payments every month to pay it off faster. This gave me flexibility—if I lost my job, I only owed the lower 30-year payment. If you can comfortably afford the 15-year payment and still have an emergency fund, go for it.

What credit score do I need to buy a house?

It depends on the loan type. Generally, you can get a conventional loan with a score around 620. FHA loans might allow scores as low as 580 (or sometimes lower with larger down payments). However, your credit score directly impacts your interest rate.

The higher your score (740+), the better the rate you will get. My advice: spend 6 months paying down credit card balances and fixing errors on your credit report before you apply for a mortgage. It can save you tens of thousands of dollars in the long run. I know waiting is frustrating, but the financial payoff is massive.

4. The Real Cost of a Mortgage

What are closing costs, and why are they so expensive?

When I bought my first house, I was shocked by closing costs. They are a collection of fees required to finalize the loan and transfer the property. They usually range from 2% to 5% of the home's purchase price. This includes the lender's origination fee, the appraisal fee, title search and insurance, and recording fees.

I highly recommend asking your lender for a "Loan Estimate" as early as possible. By law, they must provide it within 3 days of your application. Review it carefully and ask about every single fee you don't understand. Don't be afraid to negotiate; some fees are set in stone, but others can be reduced.

What is an escrow account and why did my payment go up?

An escrow account is a way for your lender to manage your property taxes and homeowners insurance. Instead of you having to remember to pay a massive tax bill once a year, your lender collects a portion of those costs every month along with your mortgage payment.

Here is the annoying part: your payment can go up if your property taxes or insurance premiums increase. The lender will do an "escrow analysis" and adjust your monthly payment to cover the shortage. It's frustrating, but it's a normal part of homeownership. Always keep a little buffer in your monthly budget for escrow increases.

What is amortization?

Amortization is the schedule of how your loan is paid off over time. It is the secret that the banks don't want you to look at too closely. In the early years of a mortgage, almost all of your monthly payment goes toward interest, and very little goes toward the principal.

For example, in the first year of a 30-year loan, you might pay $18,000 but only reduce your loan balance by $3,000. Knowing this is crucial if you want to make extra payments to save on interest. We built an Amortization Schedule tool right into our homepage calculator so you can see this for yourself.

5. The Final Steps

What is a home appraisal, and what happens if it comes in low?

An appraisal is an independent assessment of the home's value, required by the lender to ensure they aren't lending you more money than the house is worth. If you agree to buy a house for $400,000 but the appraisal comes in at $390,000, the lender will only base your loan on the $390,000.

You have to figure out how to cover the $10,000 gap. You can renegotiate with the seller, or pay the difference out of pocket. Always have an "appraisal contingency" in your purchase contract to protect yourself. Don't waive this contingency unless you have a massive amount of cash and are willing to risk it.

Can I make extra payments to pay off my mortgage faster?

Yes, and I highly encourage it. If you have a conventional loan, you can usually make extra payments without a penalty. Just make sure you specify to your lender that the extra money should go directly to the principal, not just towards next month's payment.

Paying an extra $100 a month towards your principal can shave years off your mortgage and save you tens of thousands of dollars in interest. I started doing this in my third year of homeownership, and the impact on my loan balance was shocking.

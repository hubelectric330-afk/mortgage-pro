How Much House Can You Afford?

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

How lenders decide how much you can borrow

Lenders look at the share of your gross monthly income that goes to debt. This is the debt-to-income ratio, or DTI. The front-end ratio counts only housing costs: principal, interest, taxes, insurance, PMI and HOA dues. The back-end ratio counts housing plus all other monthly debt payments, such as car loans, student loans and minimum credit card payments. A common rule of thumb is housing at or below 28% of income and total debt at or below 36%. Many loans allow more: back-end ratios up to about 43% are common, and some approvals reach about 45% to 50% with strong credit, savings or other compensating factors.

Lenders also verify income and employment, review credit, and look at your assets and down payment. Approval limits are about risk to the lender, not about what is comfortable for your life.

Other debts shrink your purchasing power

Every dollar of monthly debt reduces the payment that fits under the ratio. At 6.5% over 30 years, each $100 of monthly debt payments reduces the loan you can support by about $15,821. A $400 monthly car payment, for instance, can cut the loan capacity by well over $60,000. Paying down or avoiding debt before applying can raise what you can borrow.

Costs the ratios miss

  • Maintenance and repairs. A frequently used rule of thumb is to budget roughly 1% to 2% of the home’s value a year.
  • Utilities, internet and insurance upgrades. Often higher than in a rental.
  • Tax and insurance increases. They can raise your payment even on a fixed-rate loan. See escrow explained.
  • Furnishing and moving.
  • Savings goals. Retirement, children’s expenses and an emergency fund.

Build your own budget

Start with take-home pay, not gross pay. List fixed bills, groceries, transport, insurance, saving and spending you want to keep. What remains is the space for a housing payment that includes taxes, insurance and maintenance. Many households set a personal limit below the lender’s maximum. If a payment only works when nothing goes wrong, it is too high.

Stress tests

  • Rate test. Rerun the calculator at a rate one point higher.
  • Income test. Ask what happens if one income drops for six months.
  • Cost test. Add a repair bill of several thousand dollars in year one.
  • Tax and insurance test. Use the higher end of local estimates.

If the plan survives these tests, you have a margin.

Down payment, closing costs and reserves

Affordability is not only the monthly payment. You also need the down payment, closing costs (commonly about 2% to 5% of the price; see our closing costs guide) and money left over afterward. Many lenders want to see several months of reserves for the payment, and your own budget should include an emergency fund as well.

Pre-qualification vs pre-approval

Pre-qualification is a quick estimate based on information you provide. Pre-approval involves documents and a credit check and gives a stronger indication of what a lender may offer, though it is not a final commitment. Getting pre-approved before house hunting can prevent surprises, but remember that the amount offered is a ceiling.

How to improve what you can afford

  • Pay down debts that carry monthly payments.
  • Raise your credit score before applying (see our guide to credit scores).
  • Increase your down payment to lower the payment and PMI.
  • Compare lenders and loan types.
  • Consider a longer term for a lower required payment, with optional extra payments (see 15 vs 30 years).
  • Look at lower-priced areas or homes that need fewer repairs.

A short checklist

  • Compute your front-end and back-end ratios.
  • Decide your own comfortable payment.
  • Include taxes, insurance, PMI, HOA and maintenance.
  • Run higher-rate and lower-income tests.
  • Confirm you can pay the down payment and closing costs and still hold reserves.
  • Compare Loan Estimates from several lenders.

The most useful number is not the largest loan approved but the payment you can carry through good and bad years.

What changes with a smaller down payment

With 5% down and PMI at a hypothetical 0.75%, the same $2,100 housing limit supports a loan of about $236,958 and a price of about $249,429 at 6.5%, compared with the 20%-down price in the previous table. A smaller down payment gets you in sooner, but the PMI and larger loan reduce the price you can afford for a given payment. See our PMI guide.

Common questions

  • Is 28/36 a rule? It is a traditional guideline, not a law. Programs differ. FHA guidelines, for example, are commonly cited with higher ratios.
  • Should I borrow the maximum? Not necessarily. The maximum is a ceiling set by risk models.
  • Does renting prove I can afford a mortgage? Rent is a useful comparison, but owning adds taxes, insurance, repairs and larger one-time costs.
  • Will my other debts matter? Yes. They count in your back-end ratio.
  • Does a co-borrower help? Income may help, but both people’s debts and credit are considered.

Documents lenders usually ask for

Expect to provide recent pay stubs, tax returns or W-2 forms, bank and investment statements, identification, and details about your debts. Self-employed borrowers are often asked for more years of tax returns and business records. Gathering these early speeds up pre-approval and shows you what the lender sees. Do not open new credit accounts, make large purchases or move money between accounts without a record, because any of these can complicate approval.

Common affordability mistakes

  • Treating the lender’s maximum as the target.
  • Leaving out maintenance, insurance increases and utilities.
  • Spending the entire savings on the down payment, leaving no reserves.
  • Ignoring how other debts reduce your borrowing power.
  • Assuming income will rise to make the payment easier later.

Next step

Once you have a personal budget number, enter it in the calculator and work backward to a price range. Then talk to lenders, compare Loan Estimates and choose a home within the range that keeps your finances resilient.

Keep the margin for life events

Careers, families and health change over the years a mortgage lasts. A payment that fits today can feel heavy after a pay cut, a new child or a long illness, and the loan will not shrink to match. Leaving room in your budget for these changes is the surest protection. If you are unsure, choose the lower end of your range, keep three to six months of expenses saved after closing, and review your budget each year. A smaller payment gives you the freedom to save, handle repairs and still enjoy the home you buy.

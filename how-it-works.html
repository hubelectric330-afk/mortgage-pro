How It Works: The Real Math Behind Your Mortgage

No jargon. No hidden agendas. Just the straight facts about how we calculate your payment, and why the banks hope you never understand it.

Published by the SmartLoanCalc Founder | Updated November 2026

The purpose of this page

Most online calculators ask you to trust a number without showing how it was produced. This page explains, step by step, how SmartLoanCalc turns the figures you enter into a monthly payment, a payment breakdown, an amortization schedule and an extra-payment estimate. You can check every step yourself with a spreadsheet or a handheld calculator. If you ever find a result that does not match, please tell us through the contact page so we can look into it.

Everything on the calculator runs in your browser. The numbers you type are used to produce the results on your screen and are not sent to our servers.

The core formula

The payment on a standard fixed-rate mortgage comes from the amortization formula: M = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1). In this formula M is the monthly payment of principal and interest, P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate and n is the total number of monthly payments. The monthly rate is the annual rate divided by 12, so 6.5% becomes 0.065 ÷ 12, or about 0.0054167. The number of payments is the term in years times 12, so a 30-year loan has 360 payments.

The formula finds the single level payment that exactly pays off the loan, including interest, by the final payment. If the interest rate is zero, the payment is simply the loan amount divided by the number of payments. In a spreadsheet you can get the same figure with the PMT function: =PMT(rate/12, years*12, -loan).

Worked example with the default numbers

The calculator opens with a $400,000 home price, a $80,000 down payment (20%), a 30-year term and a 6.5% rate. The loan amount is price minus down payment, or $320,000. Applying the formula gives a principal-and-interest payment of about $2,022.62. Property tax of $4,800 a year adds $400 a month, home insurance of $1,500 a year adds $125 a month, and HOA dues are $0 in the default. The total estimated monthly payment is about $2,547.62.

The first month shows how the payment splits. Interest is the balance times the monthly rate: $320,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $1,733.33. The rest of the payment, $289.28, reduces principal, so the balance after the first payment is $319,710.72. In month two interest is charged on that lower balance, so slightly more goes to principal. Repeating this for 360 months pays the loan off.

What each input does

  • Home price and down payment. These set the loan amount. Changing the down payment percentage updates the dollar amount and the other way around.
  • Loan term. A shorter term raises the payment and lowers total interest. Terms of 10, 15, 20 and 30 years are offered.
  • Interest rate. Enter the annual rate. Use a lender quote when you have one.
  • Property tax and insurance. Enter yearly amounts. The calculator divides them by 12 for the monthly figure.
  • HOA fees. Enter the monthly dues, if any.

How PMI is estimated

When your down payment is below 20% of the price, the calculator adds an estimate for private mortgage insurance. It uses a flat 0.5% of the loan amount per year, divided by 12. For example, with 5% down on a $400,000 home the loan is $380,000, principal and interest at 6.5% is about $2,401.86, and estimated PMI is about $158.33 a month. This is a simple estimate. Real PMI commonly ranges from about 0.3% to over 1% a year depending on credit score, loan type and down payment, and a lender quote replaces our estimate. See our PMI guide for details.

The payment breakdown and chart

The doughnut chart and the list beside it divide your total monthly payment into principal and interest, property tax, insurance, PMI when applicable and HOA dues. The chart simply displays the same numbers as the list, scaled to their share of the total. Because taxes and insurance are entered as yearly amounts, the monthly figures are exact twelfths and do not include any escrow cushion your servicer might collect (see escrow explained).

How the amortization schedule is built

The schedule groups the loan month by month, then totals each year. For every month the calculator multiplies the opening balance by the monthly rate to get interest, subtracts that from the payment to get principal and subtracts the principal from the balance. The yearly rows add up twelve months of interest and principal and show the ending balance. Because the same payment is used all the way through, the final balance reaches zero apart from tiny rounding differences. To see why early payments are mostly interest, read how amortization schedules work.

How the Extra Payment Calculator works

The extra payment tool runs the same month-by-month process but adds your extra amount to the payment each month. If you enter a lump sum, it subtracts that amount from the balance after the month you choose. The loop stops when the balance reaches zero, and the number of months it took is your new payoff time. The tool also runs the original schedule without extra payments, and the difference between the two is the time and interest saved. The total extra money you pay in is shown so you can compare the cost with the saving.

What the calculator does not do

  • It does not show your lender’s final numbers. Rates, fees, taxes and insurance differ by borrower and location.
  • It does not model adjustable rates, interest-only loans or loans with balloon payments.
  • It uses a flat PMI estimate and does not model mortgage insurance on FHA, VA or USDA loans.
  • It does not include closing costs, maintenance or utilities.
  • It does not provide advice. It is an educational estimate.

For actual figures, rely on the Loan Estimate your lender must give you within three business days of applying, and on your Closing Disclosure.

Rounding and accuracy

The calculator carries full precision through its steps and rounds only when displaying amounts. The monthly payment is shown to the nearest dollar or cent depending on the field. Lenders round payments to the cent and may handle the final payment slightly differently, so your lender’s schedule can differ from ours by small amounts. Differences larger than that usually come from escrow items, mortgage insurance, rate differences, payment dates or fees.

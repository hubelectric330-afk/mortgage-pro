Escrow Accounts Explained: Why Your Payment Goes Up

By A Regular Homebuyer | Updated November 2026 | 12 Min Read

I will never forget the letter. It arrived on a random Thursday, a plain white envelope from my mortgage servicer. I opened it expecting the usual statement, and instead I found a notice saying my monthly payment was going up by $180. No explanation. No apology. Just a new number, higher than the one I had budgeted for. I sat at my kitchen table and read the letter three times. I hadn't changed anything. I hadn't missed a payment. My interest rate was fixed. So why on earth was my payment going up?

I called my lender the next morning, and the woman on the phone was patient with me. She explained that the increase wasn't about my mortgage at all. It was about my escrow account. I had heard the word "escrow" before, but I didn't really understand what it meant. I had signed papers at closing that mentioned it, but I had been so overwhelmed by the whole process that I had nodded along and moved on. That phone call was the first time anyone actually explained it to me in plain English, and it changed the way I looked at my mortgage forever.

If you've ever gotten a letter like mine, or if you're about to buy a house and want to understand what escrow actually is, this is for you. I'm going to explain it the way I wish someone had explained it to me, without jargon and without assuming you already know what any of it means.

What an Escrow Account Actually Is

An escrow account is a separate account that your lender sets up to hold money for certain expenses related to your home. The two most common things that go into escrow are property taxes and homeowners insurance. When you make your monthly mortgage payment, part of that money goes toward your loan, and part of it goes into the escrow account. Then, when your property taxes or insurance bills come due, your lender pays them out of that account on your behalf.

Here's the part that confused me at first. I was paying my property taxes and insurance through escrow without realizing it. When I thought about my mortgage payment, I thought of it as one number. But it was actually several numbers bundled together. There was the principal and interest, which is the loan itself. And then there was the escrow portion, which covered taxes and insurance. The escrow portion is why my payment went up. My taxes and insurance had changed, not my loan.

Your mortgage payment is not one number. It's at least three: principal, interest, and escrow. The first two are fixed. The third one changes. That's why your payment can go up even when your interest rate doesn't.

Why the Bank Uses Escrow in the First Place

When my lender first explained escrow to me, I was suspicious. Why does the bank get to hold my money? Why can't I just pay my own taxes and insurance directly? The answer is actually pretty simple. Property taxes are a big deal. If you don't pay them, the government can put a lien on your house, and eventually, they can take it. Homeowners insurance is also a big deal. If you don't have it, a single fire could wipe out the value of the home your lender has a stake in.

Your lender has a financial interest in your house. They want to make sure the taxes get paid and the insurance stays in place. If they let you handle it yourself and you forget or fall behind, they could lose a lot of money. Escrow protects them. It's not a scam and it's not a punishment. It's just a way for the lender to make sure the obligations tied to the house are always handled.

Once I understood that, escrow stopped feeling like a mystery and started feeling like a tool. It also meant I didn't have to worry about saving for a huge tax bill every year. The money was already being set aside every month. That part, honestly, was convenient. I didn't love the increase, but I did love not getting surprised by a tax bill.

The Real Reason My Payment Went Up

When I called my lender, the woman explained exactly what had happened. My property taxes had gone up, and my homeowners insurance premium had gone up too. The escrow account was set up based on the previous year's estimates, and those estimates turned out to be too low. The account was short on money, and the lender had to raise my monthly payment to make up the difference and rebuild a small cushion.

This is the part that made me feel a little foolish. My taxes and insurance had gone up because the value of my home had gone up. The county reassessed it, and the new value was higher. That meant my property tax bill was higher. And my insurance premium had increased because of inflation and rising costs in the insurance market. Neither of those changes was a surprise the lender sprung on me. Both were predictable consequences of owning a home. But I hadn't been paying attention, so the increase felt like it came out of nowhere.

Here's what the new breakdown looked like for my family.

Part of Payment Before Increase After Increase
Principal & Interest $1,620 $1,620
Property Taxes (escrow) $310 $420
Homeowners Insurance (escrow) $130 $200
Total Monthly Payment $2,060 $2,240

My loan didn't change at all. My interest rate didn't move. The only thing that changed was the escrow portion, and that's because taxes and insurance went up. My payment increased by $180, and every cent of that increase went to cover taxes and insurance, not the bank's profit.

The important thing to understand: When your payment goes up because of escrow, that money isn't going to the bank. It's going into an account to pay your taxes and insurance. The bank is required by law to send you an escrow analysis statement that explains the change. Read it carefully. If something doesn't look right, call and ask questions. You have a right to know where your money is going.

How the Escrow Analysis Actually Works

Once a year, your lender is required to do an escrow analysis. This is a review of how much money went into the account, how much went out, and whether the account is on track to cover the next year's bills. If the analysis shows that the account is short, your payment goes up. If the analysis shows there's extra money, your payment can go down, or you might get a refund check.

The analysis also builds in a cushion. Federal law generally allows lenders to keep a buffer of about two months of escrow payments in the account at all times. That cushion is there to protect against a sudden jump in taxes or insurance. It's a good thing in principle, but it does mean your payment can include a little extra beyond what's strictly needed for the bills themselves.

I remember getting my first escrow analysis statement and feeling overwhelmed by all the numbers. There were columns and rows and projections and shortfalls. But when I sat down and actually read it slowly, it made sense. The statement showed me what the lender expected to collect, what they expected to pay out, and what the new payment needed to be to keep things balanced. It wasn't a mystery. It was just math. And once I understood the math, I felt like I had control again.

What You Can Do When Your Payment Goes Up

When my payment went up, my first instinct was to feel helpless. But over time, I learned that there are actually several things you can do when your escrow causes an increase.

1. Read the escrow analysis carefully.

Your lender has to send you a statement that explains the change. Read it. Look at the new tax and insurance estimates. Compare them to what you were paying before. If something looks wrong, call and ask. Sometimes there's an error, and sometimes there isn't. But you can't know if you don't look.

2. Shop around for insurance.

This was the biggest surprise for me. I had just renewed my homeowners insurance every year without question. But when my premium went up, I called a couple of insurance agents and found a policy with the same coverage for significantly less. If your premium goes up, take some time to shop. You might save more than you expect, and that lower premium will lower your escrow payment.

3. Check your property tax assessment.

If your property taxes went up, it's often because the county reassessed your home. Sometimes those assessments are accurate, but sometimes they're not. You can appeal your property tax assessment, and it's more common to succeed than most people realize. If the assessment is wrong, your tax bill can be adjusted, which lowers your escrow payment.

4. Pay the escrow shortfall upfront if you can.

When there's a shortfall in the escrow account, the lender often spreads the repayment over twelve months, which is why the monthly payment goes up. But you can usually choose to pay the shortfall in one lump sum if you have the cash. That way, your monthly payment only increases to cover the higher ongoing bills, not the past shortfall. It can be a big difference.

5. Consider paying taxes and insurance yourself.

This is a bigger decision, but some lenders allow you to waive escrow and pay your own taxes and insurance directly. It means you're responsible for saving the money and making the payments on time. Some people like this because they have more control over their money. Others prefer the convenience of escrow. It's a personal choice, and you should ask your lender what your options are before you decide.

An escrow increase isn't a punishment. It's information. It's telling you that the cost of owning your home has gone up. Once you understand that, you can start looking for ways to manage it, and sometimes you can actually bring it back down.

What I Wish Someone Had Told Me at Closing

Looking back, I wish someone had sat me down at closing and explained escrow in plain English. Here's what I would have wanted to know.

  • Your monthly payment is not fixed. The principal and interest part is fixed, but the escrow part can change every year. Plan for that.
  • Read your escrow analysis every year. It's a short document that explains exactly why your payment changed. It's not scary once you know what you're looking at.
  • Shop your insurance every year or two. Loyalty doesn't always save you money. Sometimes switching saves hundreds of dollars a year, and that directly lowers your escrow payment.
  • Know that an increase isn't a sign of trouble. It's a sign that the cost of your taxes or insurance went up. It's normal, and it's manageable.
  • You have options. You can appeal your property tax assessment, shop for insurance, pay a shortfall upfront, or waive escrow entirely. You're not stuck.

How I Handle My Escrow Now

Today, I do a few simple things to keep my escrow from catching me off guard. First, I open every escrow analysis statement as soon as it arrives and read it carefully. Second, I check my homeowners insurance every year and shop around every two years. Third, I keep a small cushion in my savings account specifically for escrow increases, so if the payment goes up, I'm not scrambling to adjust my budget. Fourth, I call my lender whenever something doesn't make sense. They've been patient with me, and I've learned a lot by asking questions.

Those habits have made a real difference. My payment still goes up from time to time, but I'm not surprised by it anymore. I understand why it happens, and I know what to do about it. That feeling of understanding is worth more than the money itself. It's the difference between feeling helpless and feeling in control.

Escrow is not a fee. It's not a scam. It's just the way your lender collects money to pay your taxes and insurance on your behalf. Once you understand it, you can manage it. And once you can manage it, it stops being scary.

If you got a letter in the mail like I did, don't panic. Open it. Read it. Call your lender if you have questions. Understanding what's happening is the first step toward doing something about it. It took me a while to get there, but once I did, my mortgage stopped feeling like a mystery and started feeling like something I could actually handle.

That's my story. I hope it helps you understand your escrow and feel a little less surprised when the next letter arrives.

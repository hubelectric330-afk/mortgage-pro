15-Year vs 30-Year Mortgages Compared

By SmartLoanCalc | Last reviewed November 2026 | 7 min read

The basic trade-off

The term you choose sets the payment, the total interest and how much flexibility you keep. A shorter term means you repay the same loan faster, so each payment is larger but far less of the total is interest. A longer term lowers the required payment but charges interest for more years. Neither is automatically better: the right choice depends on your income stability, savings, other goals and risk tolerance.

The numbers: one loan, three terms

The table uses a $300,000 loan at the same 6.5% rate for every term so you can see the effect of the term alone.

TermMonthly principal and interestTotal interest paidInterest as % of loan
30-year at 6.5%$1,896$382,633128%
20-year at 6.5%$2,237$236,81379%
15-year at 6.5%$2,613$170,39857%

Moving from 30 to 15 years raises the payment by about $717 a month and cuts total interest from about $382,633 to about $170,398. That is a difference of roughly $212,235.

Real quotes usually differ by term. Shorter terms often carry a lower interest rate. At a hypothetical 5.75% on the 15-year loan, the payment would be about $2,491 and total interest about $148,421. Always compare actual quotes, not assumptions.

Cash flow and risk

A 30-year loan keeps more cash available each month. That cash can cover emergencies, a job gap, repairs, childcare or retirement contributions. A higher required payment is a fixed obligation: if your income drops, the lender still expects it. A common approach is to choose the term whose payment you can pay comfortably even if your income dips, and to keep several months of expenses in reserve.

Also consider how the larger payment affects what you can borrow. Lenders count the payment in your debt-to-income ratio, so a 15-year loan can reduce the price you qualify for compared with a 30-year loan.

Equity and interest

Both loans charge interest on the remaining balance, but a 15-year loan reduces the balance much faster. On a 30-year loan the early payments are mostly interest (see how amortization schedules work). If you plan to sell in a few years, a 30-year loan will have built little equity from payments alone, though price changes can matter more than paydown.

The opportunity cost question

Some borrowers choose a 30-year loan and invest the difference in payments. Whether that beats paying down the mortgage depends on the investment return compared with the mortgage rate, taxes and your discipline. Investment returns are not guaranteed, while paying down a mortgage gives a certain saving equal to the loan interest rate. If you are choosing between prepaying and investing, compare the mortgage rate with the after-tax return you realistically expect and your tolerance for risk, and keep an emergency fund first.

A middle path: a 30-year loan with optional extra payments

Another approach is to take the 30-year loan for its flexibility and send extra principal when your budget allows. On the $300,000 example, paying an extra $300 a month on top of the $1,896 payment would pay off the loan in about 20 years and 10 months and save roughly $135,115 in interest compared with the full 30 years. You keep the option to return to the lower required payment if money gets tight.

Before relying on this, confirm that the loan has no prepayment penalty and that extra payments are applied to principal. See our extra payment guide.

What about 20 years?

A 20-year term sits between the two. At the same rate it costs less interest than a 30-year loan and has a smaller payment increase than a 15-year loan. If a 15-year payment feels tight but you want faster payoff, ask lenders to quote a 20-year loan as well.

Refinancing later

You can change terms later by refinancing, but that costs closing fees and depends on future rates and your credit. A refinance restarts the repayment clock unless you choose a shorter term. See our refinancing guide for break-even math.

How to choose

  • 15-year may fit if: your income is stable, you have a full emergency fund, you are on track for retirement and the payment fits comfortably.
  • 30-year may fit if: your budget is tight, income varies, or you want flexibility for other goals.
  • Consider 20 years or a 30-year with extra payments if: you want a middle option.

Run each term in the calculator with your own rate, taxes, insurance and PMI, and compare the total monthly cost, not just principal and interest.

How the term affects what you can borrow

Lenders limit total monthly debt as a share of gross income. Suppose the principal-and-interest amount you can carry within that limit is $2,000 a month. At 6.5%, that supports a loan of about $316,422 over 30 years, but only about $229,593 over 15 years. The shorter term is cheaper in total interest, but it cuts the price range available to you. If you are buying near the top of your budget, the 30-year loan may be the only practical choice, with extra payments as an option.

Three hypothetical households

These are illustrations, not real cases or advice.

  • Steady two-income household with a full emergency fund. A 15-year loan may fit if the payment leaves room for retirement saving and other goals.
  • Freelancer with variable income. A 30-year loan with voluntary extra payments in strong months keeps the required payment low in slow months.
  • Buyer close to retirement. Compare the payoff date with the retirement date. A shorter term may remove the payment before income falls, but it should not drain savings needed for living costs.

Common misconceptions

  • “Interest is wasted money.” Interest is the price of borrowing and of keeping flexibility. The question is whether the extra cost is worth the lower payment for you.
  • “A 15-year loan always wins.” It saves interest, but it also concentrates your money in the house and raises the required payment.
  • “The mortgage interest deduction tips the choice.” It only helps if you itemize deductions, and many households now take the standard deduction. Ask a tax professional about your situation.
  • “I can just refinance later.” Future rates, home values and your credit are uncertain, and refinancing costs money.

Questions to ask lenders

  • What rate and fees do you quote for 30-, 20- and 15-year terms, on the same day?
  • Is there a prepayment penalty?
  • How are extra payments applied, and how do I designate them to principal?
  • What are the points and credits in each Loan Estimate?
  • How does each term change my required monthly payment and reserves?

Compare the answers on the Loan Estimate forms side by side and use the calculator to test each option with your taxes, insurance and PMI.

How fast the balance falls

Equity from payments depends heavily on the term. Using the same $300,000 loan at 6.5% for both terms, here is what you would still owe.

30-year balance15-year balance
After 5 years$280,833$230,151
After 10 years$254,328$133,563

After ten years the 15-year borrower owes about $133,563, versus about $254,328 on the 30-year loan. That difference is the benefit of the higher payment, and it is also money you cannot spend elsewhere. Home prices can rise or fall, so balance paydown is only one part of the equity picture.

A simple way to decide

  • Step 1. Get same-day quotes for 30-, 20- and 15-year terms and compare the Loan Estimates.
  • Step 2. Check the payment against your budget after retirement saving, an emergency fund and insurance, taxes and repairs.
  • Step 3. Ask what happens to your finances if income drops by a fifth for six months. If the required payment would be unmanageable, lean toward a longer term.
  • Step 4. If you want faster payoff, consider a longer term with voluntary extra payments rather than a locked-in higher payment.
  • Step 5. Revisit the choice if rates, income or goals change.

There is no universally right answer. The best loan is the one you can pay comfortably through good and bad years while still meeting your other goals.

Keep the decision in context

The term is one part of a bigger plan. A lower rate, a larger down payment, a cheaper home or fewer fees can improve your finances as much as the term itself. Compare total cost, risk and flexibility together, and revisit the numbers whenever your income, savings or rates change materially.
